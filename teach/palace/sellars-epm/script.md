## U0 · Opening  
_252 words · ≈1.7 min_

In 1956 Wilfrid Sellars published a long essay called Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind. It gave philosophy two phrases that are now common coin: the Myth of the Given, and the logical space of reasons. Its target is not a single doctrine but a whole way of construing knowledge, which Sellars calls the framework of givenness. On that framework, some episodes of awareness are simply had, and yet already count as knowing, so that everything else we know can rest on them.

> **stop 1** · a carved foundation stone set into the base of an old stone building · _framework of givenness_

Richard Rorty's introduction to this edition names three works that turned analytic philosophy away from its positivist starting point: Wittgenstein's Philosophical Investigations, Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism, and this essay. Quine, by attacking the distinction between analytic and synthetic truths, helped destroy the rationalist form of foundationalism. Sellars, by attacking the distinction between what is given to the mind and what is added by the mind, helped destroy the empiricist form.

> **stop 2** · photograph of Ludwig Wittgenstein as a young man, around 1930 · _Two Dogmas_

The essay moves in three phases. First comes a critique of givenness, through sense data, the logic of looks, and the question whether empirical knowledge has a foundation. Then a turn to science and its relation to common sense. Finally, a myth of Sellars' own, about a genius named Jones, meant to show how talk of inner episodes can be public and yet privileged. Three questions should stay open throughout: what exactly is wrong with the given; what could make an observation authoritative, if not givenness; and what inner episodes are, if not objects of an inner gaze.

## U1 · Part I: The framework of givenness  
_1324 words · ≈8.8 min_

Sellars begins by conceding what no one denies. There is a difference between inferring that something is the case and seeing it to be the case. If the given meant only what is observed, the existence of data would be uncontroversial. But the phrase, as a piece of professional epistemological shoptalk, in his words "carries a substantial theoretical commitment." Many things have been called given: sense contents, material objects, universals, propositions, real connections, first principles. The target is not any one of these but the framework within which they are called given, and Sellars finds it nearly everywhere. Few philosophers have been free of it: "certainly not Kant, and, I would argue, not even Hegel, that great foe of “immediacy.”" Sense data come first only in order of treatment. He attacks them, in his words, "only as a first step in a general critique of the entire framework of givenness." The living targets are the sense-datum epistemologists of his day, C. I. Lewis and H. H. Price among them.

> **stop 3** · portrait of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel by Jakob Schlesinger, 1831 · _the given_

Sections two through four lay out the classical sense-datum theory. It distinguishes an act of awareness, called sensing, from its object, such as a color patch. Being a sense datum is then a relational property of whatever is sensed, and Sellars adopts the neutral term sense content for the item itself. The first logical point follows at once. The given was meant to explain how empirical knowledge rests on a foundation of non-inferential knowledge. But, in Sellars' words, "what is known, even in non-inferential knowledge, is facts rather than particulars". Sense-datum theorists say that particulars are sensed. So they face a dilemma. Either particulars are sensed and sensing is not knowing, in which case sense data by themselves imply no knowledge at all; or sensing is knowing, in which case what is sensed are facts, not particulars.

> **stop 4** · a plate from the Munsell Book of Color showing graded red color chips · _facts, not particulars_

The classical theorist wants both horns: sensing is a knowing, and its objects are particulars. He "seeks to have his cake and eat it," Sellars says, and shows how this can be done without outright nonsense, by using the word know in two senses. A sense content is said to be known when some fact about it, such as that it is red, is non-inferentially known. Ordinary usage lends cover, since we ask whether someone knows John, meaning whether they are acquainted with him. Russell's knowledge by acquaintance is, on this account, a useful metaphor that has congealed into a technical term.

Section five shows what is at stake. Some theorists made sensing an unanalyzable act, severing the link from sensing to knowing. Others, from Moore's Refutation of Idealism until about 1938, analyzed sensing in non-epistemic terms. If that analysis coincided with the analysis of non-inferential knowing, then "The entailment which was thrown out the front door would have sneaked in by the back."

> **stop 5** · Dutch Golden Age still life with a half-eaten pie on a pewter plate, by Pieter Claesz · _knowledge by acquaintance_

Then comes an aside that the whole essay will later cash. The idea that epistemic facts can be analyzed without remainder into non-epistemic facts, whether phenomenal or behavioral, public or private, is, Sellars says, "a radical mistake—a mistake of a piece with the so-called “naturalistic fallacy” in ethics." He declines to press the point here and promises it will be central later. The promise is paid in section thirty-six, where to call an episode a knowing is to place it in the logical space of reasons rather than to describe it empirically. Brandom reads this irreducibility of the epistemic, and more broadly the normative, to the descriptive as the core of the essay; readers who take Sellars' scientific realism as the main lesson weigh it differently. For now, what matters is that classical theorists of both kinds took givenness to be fundamental in another sense: it presupposes no learning.

> **stop 6** · photograph of the philosopher G. E. Moore in middle age · _naturalistic fallacy_

Section six turns that assumption into a trap. The classical theorist treats sensing as unlearned: a newborn, alive and kicking, senses, while a person knocked on the head does not. But most empirically minded philosophers also hold that all classificatory consciousness, all knowing that something is thus-and-so, involves learning and concept formation. The result is what Sellars calls an inconsistent triad. Proposition A: that x senses red sense content s entails that x non-inferentially knows that s is red. Proposition B: the ability to sense sense contents is unacquired. Proposition C: the ability to know facts of the form x is phi is acquired. Any two entail the denial of the third. A and B together make a kind of classificatory knowing unlearned, contradicting C. B and C make sensing unlearned and knowing learned, so sensing cannot be knowing, contradicting A. A and C make sensing a knowing and knowing learned, so sensing must be learned, contradicting B.

> **stop 7** · the Penrose impossible triangle drawn as a three-dimensional solid · _inconsistent triad_

One proposition must go, and each exit has its price. Abandon A, and sensing becomes a non-cognitive fact. It may still be a necessary condition of non-inferential knowledge, even a logically necessary one, but it cannot constitute that knowledge, and the sense datum stops doing foundational epistemic work. Abandon B, and sensing can be a learned knowing, but the price is cutting the concept of a sense datum off from its supposed common-sense counterparts: sensations, feelings, afterimages, tickles and itches. Abandon C, and one keeps an unlearned knowing, but, in Sellars' words, "to abandon C is to do violence to the predominantly nominalistic proclivities of the empiricist tradition." Brandom's study guide warns that this brisk dismissal can mislead readers into thinking Sellars is speaking as an empiricist. His own reasons for holding onto C are not loyalty to the tradition; they arrive in section twenty-nine as psychological nominalism.

> **stop 8** · an old wooden fingerpost signpost with three arms pointing different ways at a country crossroads · _three exits_

Section seven diagnoses how the classical theorist got here. The classical concept of a sense datum, Sellars suggests, is "a mongrel resulting from a crossbreeding of two ideas". The first is the idea of inner episodes, sensations of red or of C sharp, which occur in humans and brutes without prior learning, and without which one could not see that a surface is red and triangular. This idea arises in explaining perception in scientific style: why it can be for someone as though they were seeing a red triangle when nothing red or triangular is there. Nothing in it makes sensation cognitive. The second is the idea of inner episodes that are non-inferential knowings that items are red or C sharp, and that serve as the evidence for all other empirical propositions.

> **stop 9** · photograph of a scruffy mixed-breed dog sitting in a street · _mongrel_

The second idea is driven by a worry. Ostensible seeings include non-veridical ones, with no inspectable hallmark of the veridical, so a foundation made of them seems too precarious. Sensations appear to have exactly the virtues ostensible seeings lack, and three confusions weld them to the second idea. The phrase "sensation of a red triangle" has the grammar of "thought of a celestial city", suggesting sensations are cognitive. Sensations seem closer to the mind, easier to get at, than physical surfaces. Above all, it strikes these philosophers that "it doesn’t make sense to speak of unveridical sensations", overlooking that if an experience cannot be unveridical, it cannot be veridical either. The upshot is the idea that "a sensation of a red triangle is the very paradigm of empirical knowledge."

> **stop 10** · engraving of the Celestial City from an illustrated edition of Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress · _ostensible seeing_

Sellars will not simply discard either strand. The first idea he calls legitimate, though not without its puzzles; it returns, rehabilitated, in sections sixty through sixty-two, where impressions are theoretical states posited to explain perception. The second is the one the critique of foundations takes apart, in sections thirty-two through thirty-eight. Two later readings frame the stakes. Rorty, in Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature, generalizes the attack into a critique of privileged representations as such. McDowell, in Mind and World, casts the modern debate as an oscillation between the Given and a coherentism that loses friction with the world; whether Sellars himself escapes that oscillation is disputed between McDowell and Brandom. Before separating the two ideas, though, Sellars takes a detour through a heterodox view, on which sense-datum talk is not a theory of anything at all.

## U2 · Part II: Another language?  
_515 words · ≈3.4 min_

Part two examines a heterodox proposal, which Sellars associates with Ayer: that sense-datum talk is another language, contrived by the epistemologist for situations the plain man describes by saying that the book now looks green to him. On this view the vocabulary of sense data adds no descriptive content. A sentence of the form "x presents S with a phi sense datum" is simply stipulated to have the force of "x looks phi to S". So "The tomato presents S with a bulgy red sense-datum" means exactly what "The tomato looks red and bulgy to S" means, because it was stipulated to. Sellars then builds a picture of a code: a system of symbols, each standing for a whole sentence, whose parts are not themselves symbols, and whose logical relations are, in his words, "completely parasitical" on those of the sentences represented.

> **stop 11** · botanical illustration of a ripe red tomato on the vine · _another language_

The code can be enriched. Its symbols might contain mnemonic parts, flags whose letters remind us of features of the sentences they stand for. Sense-datum sentences could be such flags, with "sense datum" reminding us that the represented sentence contains "looks," and "red" that it contains "looks red." But taken seriously, this means there are no independent logical relations among sense-datum sentences, and words like "all," "some," "red," and "determinate" inside them lose "the full-blooded status of their counterparts in ordinary usage." The clues can mislead. From "Some elephants are pink" one may infer that some elephants have a determinate shade of pink. But the right to fly the flag "Some sense data are pink" carries no right to fly "Some sense data have a determinate shade of pink," since, as Sellars will argue about looks, something can look pink without looking any determinate shade.

> **stop 12** · chart of international maritime signal flags in bright colors · _code flags_

Section nine b draws the decisive contrast. One is constantly tempted to treat the flags as sentences in a theory, coordinated with ordinary perception talk as molecule talk is coordinated with talk of gas pressure. Codes and theories do share something: both are contrived, and both are under the control of the language they are coordinated with. That shared feature lends the another-language view its plausibility. But within that control, Sellars says, "the theoretical language has an autonomy which contradicts the very idea of a code." The another-language theorist wants it both ways, drawing on the fact that codes have no surplus value, and on the fact that theories explain. The two are incompatible, "for it is just because theories have “surplus value” that they can provide explanations."

> **stop 13** · Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 figure of gas particles in a cylinder under a weighted piston, from Hydrodynamica · _surplus value_

This contrast returns in section sixty-two, where the language of impressions that Jones teaches is indeed another language, but one with an autonomous logical structure, containing an explanation of, and not merely a code for, how things look. Meanwhile, Sellars notes, the another-language theorists spent their ingenuity translating physical-object statements into statements about sense contents. Any clarification a code could offer would have to come from illuminating ordinary talk of looking and appearing, and that runs into trouble once the role of "looks" is understood. In his words, "it is to an examination of this role that I now turn."

## U3 · The logic of "looks"  
_2411 words · ≈16.1 min_

Whatever sense data are, they were introduced to illuminate how things look. Part Three takes that at its word and examines "looks" directly. It is the first great constructive argument of the essay, and much of what follows depends on it.

Section ten begins with a recap. The classical sense datum was a mismating of two ideas: unlearned inner episodes, such as sensations, which make perception possible; and inner episodes that are non-inferential knowings and supply the evidence for everything else. Then Sellars widens the target. Many critics of the given assume its central mistake is the positing of private inner episodes. Sellars denies this. Suppose you reject private episodes on Wittgensteinian grounds, because language is learned publicly, among public objects, under public sanctions. You are then safe from the sense-datum form of the Myth, but not from the Myth itself. You have, in Sellars' words, no defense against "the givenness of such facts as that physical object x looks red to person S at time t."

> **stop 14** · a ripe red tomato on a white plate · _looks red_

So the Myth is not essentially about sense data, and not essentially about the inner. It can take hold of perfectly public-seeming facts about how things look. That is why "looks" deserves a whole Part.

Section eleven sets out the rival pictures. Philosophers have supposed that "The tomato looks red to Jones" asserts a three-place relation among an object, a person, and a quality, like "x is between y and z." They then ask whether this relation can be analyzed. Sense-datum theorists say yes, in terms of sense data. Others only say that looks-facts are explained by sense data. Broad asks why the penny should look elliptical unless something elliptical is before the mind. Sellars marks the difference: if the analysis were correct, no one could believe that x looks red to S without believing that S has sense data, whereas one can believe a fact without believing its explanation. Theorists of appearing, with Chisholm as the natural example, reply that looks-facts are ultimate and irreducible. Sellars agrees that "x looks red to S" does not mean that S is related to something red. But his reason will be quite different from theirs.

> **stop 15** · a worn copper penny seen at a slant, so that it appears elliptical · _triadic relation_

Section twelve is the first heavy section. Its starting point is simple. The sense of "red" in which things look red is, on the face of it, the same as the sense in which things are red. When you wonder whether something that looks red really is red, you are asking whether it has the color it appears to have. One dodge is to hyphenate the phrase, treating "looks-red" as an unbreakable unit. Sellars concedes that the dodge contains an insight, namely that looks is not a relation among a person, a thing, and a quality. But the true reason for this gives the hyphenators no comfort.

Then comes the thesis. In Sellars' words, "being red is logically prior, is a logically simpler notion, than looking red." You cannot analyze being red in terms of looking red. But this creates a puzzle. It is a necessary truth that x is red if and only if x would look red to standard observers in standard conditions. That looks like a definition of physical redness in terms of looking red.

> **stop 16** · a tailor's color swatch book fanned open to a page of reds · _logically prior_

If it is a definition, it is circular. Once "red" on the right-hand side is given its own standing, it turns out to be what it plainly is, the predicate "red" said of physical objects. So how can the equivalence be necessary if it is not a definition?

Section thirteen lays out the two-part way out. The second part will show how the equivalence can be necessary without being a definition. The first part, which is logically prior, will show that "x looks red to S" asserts no relation, whether three-place or two-place. The reason is not that the relation can be analyzed after all. It is that looks is not a relation at all. The question "Is looks a relation?" should dissolve rather than be answered.

To show this, Sellars tells a story, the most memorable in the essay. A young man named John works in a necktie shop. He has learned color words in the usual way, except that he has only ever looked at things in standard conditions. Every evening he goes over his stock, saying "This is red," "That is green," and his peers nod in approval.

> **stop 17** · a rack of silk neckties in a small old-fashioned shop window · _necktie shop_

Then electric light is invented, and John is the last to install it. Just after he does, his neighbor Jim comes in to buy a tie. John offers him a handsome green one. Jim says it isn't green and takes him outside, where it is blue. John suggests that the electric light changes the tie's color and daylight changes it back. Jim says that would be a queer kind of change. John protests that they both saw it was green inside. Jim's reply is the pivot of the story: "No, we didn’t see that it was green in there, because it wasn’t green, and you can’t see what isn’t so!"

John is left with nothing to say. The next time he holds the tie in the shop, he suppresses the impulse to say "green" and says "blue." But he doesn't see that it is blue. He is using the sentence as the conclusion of an inference, not as a report. Asked what he sees, he says: "It is as though I were seeing the necktie to be green."

> **stop 18** · an early carbon-filament electric light bulb glowing · _electric light_

Section fifteen comes back later. John now answers, "It looks green, but take it outside and see." One reading of this is the one Sellars wants to reject. On that reading John has learned to report a new kind of fact: a minimal fact that is still objective, independent of his beliefs and conceptual framework, and safer to report because he is less likely to be wrong about it. That is the looks-fact as the foundationalist wants it, the last stronghold of the given. Sellars grants that "This looks green to me now" does seem to have a reporting role. The question is what it reports, if not a minimal objective fact and not a sense datum.

The answer, in sections sixteen a and sixteen b, is the turning point of the Part. Having something look green is, as an experience, very like seeing that something is green. But seeing is not just an experience. To call an experience a seeing is to do more than describe it. In Sellars' words: "It is to characterize it as, so to speak, making an assertion or claim, and—which is the point I wish to stress—to endorse that claim."

> **stop 19** · a notary's rubber stamp pressing an inked seal onto a document · _endorsement_

The analysis follows. "Jones sees that x is green" both ascribes a propositional claim to Jones's experience and endorses it. "X looks green to Jones" ascribes the same claim but does not endorse it. "X merely looks green" rejects it. Two experiences can be identical as experiences, and yet one is properly called a seeing and the other a mere looking. So the difference between seeing and looking need not be a difference in the experience. It is a difference in the stance the speaker takes toward the claim the experience contains. When I report that x looks green, the report shows that the question whether to endorse has come up, as if in a higher court. Sellars calls such a report "not only a report, but the withholding of an endorsement." This also explains why I only say "I see that x is green," rather than simply "X is green," when that question has been raised.

> **stop 20** · an eighteenth-century courtroom with a judge's bench · _ascribe without endorsing_

Sellars knows he is spending credit. Talk of experiences containing propositional claims may seem to beg the question. He writes: "If I am permitted to issue this verbal currency now, I hope to put it on the gold standard before concluding the argument." The note is paid in section sixty and after, where Jones's theory models perceptions as inner episodes on the pattern of overt reporting, and so gives the talk of claims inside experience its footing.

Section sixteen b adds two refinements. First, seeing is not just the occurrence of the claim "this is green," even a claim wrung from the perceiver by the object. Sellars turns a simile of Kant's on its head, one Kant used in another context, where reason puts Nature to the question. Now, Sellars says, Nature "puts us to the question." The something more is what philosophers call visual impressions, and their logical status remains open until the end of the essay. Second, Ryle calls "sees" an achievement word. Sellars prefers another label: "I prefer to call it a “so it is” or “just so” word, for the root idea is that of truth."

> **stop 21** · a gold ingot stamped with an assay mark · _gold standard_

Here the seminar divides. Brandom takes these sections as a model of his own programme. On his reading, "looks" talk adds no new, more certain layer of fact. It gives explicit form to something we already do, withholding endorsement. That is his general claim about logical and quasi-logical vocabulary, which makes explicit what we are doing when we give and ask for reasons. McDowell takes something different from the same passage. His conceptualism about experience, developed in *Mind and World* and in the Woodbridge Lectures, "Having the World in View," begins from the idea that experiences themselves contain claims, so that the world can bear rationally on belief. Later, in "Avoiding the Myth of the Given," McDowell revised this: the content of experience is intuitional, not propositional. Whether section sixteen describes what experience is, as McDowell takes it, or what our talk about experience does, as Brandom takes it, remains one of the live disputes over this essay.

> **stop 22** · a wooden signpost at a fork in a country road · _making explicit_

Section seventeen collects dividends. The account treats qualitative and existential looking together, as degrees of endorsement. "The tree looks bent" endorses the part of the claim about the tree's existence and withholds the rest. "There looks to be a bent tree over there" endorses almost nothing, only that there is an over there as opposed to a here. The account also explains generic looks. A tie can look red without looking any determinate shade of red. That would be baffling if looking were a natural fact about objects, but it is easy if looking is an epistemic fact. The claim contained in the experience may simply be the determinable "This is red" rather than the determinate "This is crimson." Sellars' comparison: one can believe that Cleopatra's Needle is tall without believing it to be any particular number of feet tall.

> **stop 23** · Cleopatra's Needle, the granite obelisk on the Thames Embankment in London · _generic looks_

Section eighteen pays off the second part of the plan. The concept of looking green presupposes the concept of being green. Being green in turn involves the ability to tell colors by looking, which requires knowing in what circumstances to view an object to find out its color. John, now more sophisticated, explains that the tie looks green because it is blue and blue things look green in this light, and in daylight things look what they are. So the troubling equivalence is necessary, in Sellars' words, "not because the right-hand side is the definition of “x is red,” but because “standard conditions” means conditions in which things look what they are." The necessity comes from the meaning of "standard," not from any definition of "red." It is not a reduction, and so it is not circular. Sellars adds that, at the level of common sense, which conditions are standard is fixed by a vague, open-textured list.

> **stop 24** · a mercury thermometer and a pocket watch lying side by side on a laboratory bench · _standard conditions_

Section nineteen draws the price, and it is steep for traditional empiricism. If recognizing looks presupposes being green, and being green involves knowing which circumstances are standard, then the concepts become interdependent. You cannot tell what the circumstances are without noticing perceptible features of other things, including their colors. It would seem that one could not form the concept of green unless one already had it. Nor will it do to say that responding to green things with the noise "This is green" in standard conditions is enough. The subject must know that the conditions are appropriate. Sellars does not conclude that one must have concepts before one has them. He concludes that "one can have the concept of green only by having a whole battery of concepts of which it is one element." More sharply: "one has no concept pertaining to the observable properties of physical objects in Space and Time unless one has them all."

> **stop 25** · an artist's wooden paint box with a full row of pigment cakes · _conceptual holism_

This is the conceptual holism, and Sellars flags it as out of step with logical atomism, the view that basic observational concepts are logically independent of one another. Comparison with Quine is natural, since his "Two Dogmas" appeared only a few years earlier and is also holist, but the two holisms differ in kind. Quine's concerns confirmation: statements face the tribunal of experience as a body, not one by one. Sellars' concerns concept possession: grasping a single observational concept requires grasping many others, together with the knowledge of when observation is reliable. Brandom's inferentialism, on which a concept's content is its place in a network of inferences, takes this passage as one of its starting points. Sellars will return to it in section thirty-seven, where he asks the reader to reread section nineteen. There the holism about concepts turns out to be the same as the holism about knowledge that dissolves the regress.

> **stop 26** · a spider's web beaded with dew, every strand connected · _whole battery of concepts_

Section twenty gives the atomist a reply, and Sellars names his own project in the atomist's voice. The atomist says the logical space of physical objects rests on a more fundamental logical space, that of sense contents, whose concepts do have the independence empiricism prizes. Theoretical concepts like molecule are interdependent, but they get their empirical content by being coordinated with a more basic space. Until Sellars has disposed of that more basic space, the atomist says, "your incipient Meditations Hegeliennes are premature." Rorty and Brandom make much of this self-description. It places the essay in the line of Hegel's attack on immediacy, dressed as an empiricist's complaint.

A sense-datum theorist then presses one more point: perhaps physical properties can be analyzed directly into the qualities of sense contents, bypassing looks altogether. Sellars asks how the theorist comes by that framework. The theorist's route is always through reflection on things looking red, and the analysis of looks has so far turned up no sense contents at all. The theorist's best remaining question is still unanswered. How can something look red to me unless something red is actually present to my mind? That question opens Part Four.

> **stop 27** · a nineteenth-century lithograph portrait of Hegel · _Meditations Hegeliennes_

## U4 · Explaining looks  
_1033 words · ≈6.9 min_

Part Four takes up the sense-datum theorist's best question: how can a physical object look red to someone unless something in the situation is red, and the perceiver is taking account of it? In his words: "There is, I propose to show, something to this line of thought, though the story turns out to be a complicated one." But he warns that whatever emerges will be a sense-datum theory only in a sense stripped of its traditional epistemological force. One difficulty is immediate. If the inner item is red in the very sense in which physical objects are red, then items that differ categorically from physical objects would share one and the same redness with them. Food for thought, he says. And there is a deflationary reply: this looks red because it is an orange object seen in such and such circumstances. A good everyday explanation, but not necessarily the only one, or the most searching.

> **stop 28** · a ripe orange lit by a deep red stage light against a black background · _something red_

Section twenty-two considers two further explanations. The first comes by analogy. A balloon's expansion can be explained in two ways: by the Boyle-Charles laws, relating observable volume, pressure and temperature, or by the kinetic theory, which postulates molecules. Just so, a thing's looking red might be explained by empirical generalizations about colors, circumstances and looks, or by a theory of perception in which immediate experiences play the role of the molecules. Sellars sees the air of paradox at once. Those who appealed to immediate experiences took them to be the least theoretical of entities, "the observables par excellence." So he lays the suggestion aside until a more propitious context of thought. This is a major promissory note. The laid-aside option is exactly where the argument ends up: in Parts Fifteen and Sixteen, the mythical Jones postulates inner episodes as theoretical entities, first thoughts and then impressions.

> **stop 29** · the Montgolfier brothers' hot-air balloon ascending at Versailles, 1783 · _theoretical entities_

The second explanation holds that we can find immediate experiences by inspection, as components of lookings. To assess it, Sellars sets out three situations: seeing that x over there is red; its looking to one that x over there is red; and its looking to one as though there were a red object over there. What they share is a propositional content, the idea that x over there is red, with the qualification that in the third case the content is existential. What distinguishes them is endorsement. The seeing endorses the idea fully, the qualitative looking partially, the existential looking not at all. Then comes a residue. Beyond propositional content and degree of endorsement, what we commit ourselves to in describing these experiences includes something further, which Sellars calls the descriptive content. This too, he supposes, may be identical across all three.

> **stop 30** · a red telephone box half-hidden in thick fog on a London street · _propositional content_

Now what Sellars calls the decisive point. Looks talk specifies this common descriptive content only indirectly, by implying that if the shared propositional content were true, all three situations would be seeings. In his words: "Both existential and qualitative lookings are experiences that would be seeings if their propositional contents were true." So the grammar of looks raises questions it cannot answer. What is the intrinsic character of the descriptive content? And how can all three experiences have it, when in the first a red object must be present, in the second the object need not be red, and in the third there need be no object at all? Keep the two apart. Propositional content is what the experience claims, and it belongs with thoughts. Descriptive content is the residue, and characterizing it is the problem that Jones's impressions will eventually address.

> **stop 31** · a mirage shimmering over a straight desert highway · _descriptive content_

Section twenty-three dismisses one tempting way to characterize that residue directly, by prying the word "red" loose from physical objects. The gambit runs through surfaces. We see a table only by seeing its facing surface; a surface is a two-dimensional expanse, perhaps bulgy but without thickness; such an expanse is not a physical object, and there are even wild expanses belonging to none; so the common descriptive content is a bulgy red expanse. Sellars' verdict is that the argument rests on "a simple equivocation on the phrase 'having a red surface.'" When an object is red outside and green inside, the only particular involved is the object itself. In his words: "The fundamental grammar of the attribute red is physical object x is red at place p and at time t." Two-dimensional particulars may have a place in an ideal scientific picture, which section sixty-one will sketch, but not in an analysis of ordinary color talk, any more than Minkowski's Space-Time worms analyze what we mean by physical objects.

Seeing is an experiencing, but it does not follow that its descriptive content is an experiencing, nor that a looking is. The descriptive core may be experienced, as what is experienced rather than as the experiencing, but what kind of thing is it? We cannot call it a red experience. Finally, an obstacle is cleared. Call it not a red experience but an experience of red, and the objection returns: does a sensation of a red triangle not entail a red triangle? ## U5 · Impressions and ideas  
_1023 words · ≈6.8 min_

Section twenty-five turns historical. The tradition did assimilate sensations to thoughts: Descartes' "thought" and Locke's "idea" covered sensations and images as well as judgments and volitions. The apparatus of conceptualism was extended accordingly. Just as a thing has objective being in the thought of it, the red triangle was said to have objective being in the sensation. Sensations then differ from abstract ideas only in specificity and complexity; for Descartes they are confused thoughts of their external causes. And the conceptualist thesis that the being of abstract entities is to be conceived becomes, in Descartes and Locke and then in Berkeley, the thesis that the being of perceptible qualities is to be perceived. Sellars' judgment is brisk: "I think we would all agree, today, that this assimilation of sensations to thoughts is a mistake." But escaping this blind alley brings us no nearer a direct characterization of immediate experience.

> **stop 34** · Frans Hals' portrait of René Descartes · _objective being_

Part Six opens with an objector who says the search is needless. Just examine the experience, locate its kind, name it phi, and classify accordingly. Here, Sellars says, the Myth of the Given is being invoked, and it splits his one problem into two. First: how do we become aware of an immediate experience as of one sort, and of a simultaneous one as of another? Second, the inverted spectrum: how can I know that you attach my labels to the same sorts? Might the sort I call red be the sort you call green, systematically throughout the spectrum? On the Myth's telling, the first question is no problem at all, and the second may have no solution.

> **stop 35** · Newton's colour circle from the Opticks · _inverted spectrum_

Sellars reverses the priority. In his words: "We shall find that the second question, to be a philosophical perplexity, presupposes a certain answer to the first question—indeed the answer given by the myth." The inverted spectrum bites only if awareness of sorts is a private, primordial feature of experience, so that each of us classifies by a standard the other can never check. If awareness of sorts is instead acquired with a public language whose basic ties run to public objects, the worry loses the premise that made it a perplexity. The diagnosis rides on his answer to the first question, which comes by way of the classical empiricists.

> **stop 36** · Godfrey Kneller's portrait of John Locke · _determinate repeatables_

Today the problem of abstract ideas concerns awareness of determinate repeatables, such as a particular shade of crimson, at least as much as awareness of determinables such as color. For Locke, Berkeley and Hume, the problem concerned determinables only. Awareness of determinates was no problem. For Locke, an abstract idea of white is just a sensation of white isolated from its context, an image differing from a sensation only by being, in a modern phrase, centrally aroused. His difficulty was generic thought, and he met it with what Sellars calls an "adjunctive theory of specification": the idea of a determinate is the idea of the determinable conjoined with something further, being A and B. Notoriously, this cannot relate being red to being crimson.

> **stop 37** · John Smibert's portrait of George Berkeley · _contracted_

Berkeley is in the same position. His question was not how we get from particulars to repeatables, but how, granted awareness of absolutely specific qualities, we become conscious of genera. His answer: we have an idea of a genus only as "contracted," in the Scotist term Sellars borrows, into one of its species. An idea of triangle is always of some determinate triangle, though any one will do. Hume denies occurrent thoughts of determinables altogether, but he too presupposes awareness of determinates. All three take for granted an innate ability to be aware of determinate sorts simply by having sensations and images. That is the given in its classical empiricist form.

> **stop 38** · Allan Ramsay's portrait of David Hume in a red coat · _abstract ideas_

Section twenty-nine names Sellars' positive view. Twist Hume slightly, treating the initial elements as red particulars rather than impressions of red, and consciousness of sorts becomes an association of words like "red" with classes of resembling particulars. Everything depends on how that association is conceived. If forming it requires awareness that the particulars resemble, the given has merely moved, from crimson to the repeatable resemblance. If it requires awareness that they are red, conceptualism has only been traded for the realist version of the Myth, the classical sense-datum theory. Only if no such awareness mediates the association do we get what Sellars calls psychological nominalism, "according to which all awareness of sorts, resemblances, facts, etc., in short, all awareness of abstract entities—indeed, all awareness even of particulars—is a linguistic affair." Learning a language presupposes no such awareness at all.

> **stop 39** · title page of Samuel Johnson's Dictionary of the English Language, 1755 · _psychological nominalism_

Two remarks follow. First, the Humean version, words tied to things by association, is in Sellars' words "impossibly crude and inadequate as an account of the simplest concept." Second, once sensations are purged of epistemic aboutness, nothing forces the basic word-world tie to run between words and private experiences. It runs between "red" and red physical objects, though sensations may mediate it causally. Readers weigh the cost differently. Brandom treats psychological nominalism as a founding commitment of his view that conceptual awareness is a linguistic achievement. McDowell accepts that concepts come with initiation into language, but insists that experience itself is already conceptual. Other critics ask whether the thesis leaves room for the discriminating awareness of animals and infants.

> **stop 40** · a moonlit forest at night, nineteenth-century Romantic landscape painting · _thinking in presence_

Section thirty names a source of the Myth that catches even those suspicious of inner episodes. Borrowing Price's contrast, Sellars makes thinking in presence, not thinking in absence, the real test of a theory of language. Theories nominalist about absence often "turn out to be quite 'Augustinian' when the scalpel is turned to their account of thinking in presence." ## U6 · The logic of "means"  
_568 words · ≈3.8 min_

Part Seven answers that worry. Sellars disowns any equation of concepts with words, or accepts it "only in a relatively Pickwickian sense," and he promises to distinguish thoughts from their verbal expression later. What the term connotes is narrower. In his words: "the primary connotation of 'psychological nominalism' is the denial that there is any awareness of logical space prior to, or independent of, the acquisition of a language." The thesis concerns what comes first in acquisition. It does not say that thinking is talking.

> **stop 41** · Phiz's illustration of Mr. Pickwick addressing the Pickwick Club · _logical space_

Then comes a point of fundamental importance. "Red" would not be a predicate without predicate syntax. Nor would it be the predicate it is unless, in certain frames of mind, we tended to respond to red objects in standard circumstances with something having the force of "This is red." Once antecedent awareness of redness is abandoned, it is tempting to think these two facts are what make "red" mean red: predicate syntax, plus being a response to red things. Price, Sellars notes, rightly stigmatized this as the "thermometer view." The label's point is that a mercury column also responds reliably, yet means nothing. The view returns, revised, in section thirty-five, as an account of observation reports.

> **stop 42** · an antique glass mercury thermometer on a brass mount · _thermometer view_

The thermometer view draws its plausibility from a grammatical illusion. The German case, "rot" means red, looks like "Cowley adjoins Oxford." Assimilate "means" to a two-place relation between a word and a nonverbal thing, and the natural candidate for the relation is association. Sellars' denial is flat: such statements "are not relational statements." "Rot" could not mean red unless it were associated with red things, but the semantical statement does not say so, and it is not shorthand for a story about associations. It conveys that the mentioned word "rot" "plays the same role in a certain linguistic economy," that of German speakers, as the word "red," which is not mentioned but used, exhibited, on the right-hand side.

> **stop 43** · an old engraved map of Oxford showing the road east to Cowley · _not relational_

Compare "und" means and with "rot" means red. The first says that "und" plays the purely formal role of a logical connective; the second, that "rot" plays the role of the observation word "red." "Means" has the same sense in both, and no one need say that "und" stands in a meaning relation to Conjunction. So nothing follows from the statement that "red" means the quality red about how simple the role of "red" is. That role may be "a complicated one indeed," and understanding it may require knowledge that classical empiricism took to be only contingently tied to having empirical concepts. That is the holism of section nineteen, restated as semantics, and the 'Fido'-Fido picture of word naming object loses its hold.

> **stop 44** · a nineteenth-century oil painting of a faithful dog sitting by a door · _functional role_

Here is the seed of Sellars' later functional-role semantics, developed in "Meaning as Functional Classification," where dot quotation lets one classify any expression by the role an exhibited word plays: to say that "rot" means red is to say that "rot"s are dot-red-dots. Brandom's inferential role semantics descends from this passage, specifying roles by inferential commitments and entitlements. Referentialists object that roles alone cannot fix reference and truth conditions. Sellars' reply, already visible here, is that the role of "red" includes responsive, non-inferential uses keyed to red things. Which brings us back to observation. If part of the role of "red" is to figure in reports such as "This is red," what gives those reports their authority? Part Eight asks whether empirical knowledge has a foundation.

## U7 · Does empirical knowledge have a foundation?  
_2112 words · ≈14.1 min_

Part Seven left us with psychological nominalism: there is no awareness of logical space before the acquisition of a language, and to say what a word means is to classify its role, not to name a relation between the word and a thing. That is a thesis about concepts. Part Eight asks what it means for knowledge. This is the second great argument of the essay, and the place where the phrase "the logical space of reasons" is born.

Section thirty-two states the foundationalist picture precisely. It is the idea of a structure of particular matters of fact, each of which can be known non-inferentially and presupposes no other knowledge, whether of particular facts or general truths. The non-inferential knowledge of that structure forms, in Sellars' words, "the ultimate court of appeals for all factual claims—particular and general—about the world."

> **stop 45** · a nineteenth-century engraving of a high court in session, judges on a raised bench · _court of appeals_

Sellars then points to something in his own wording. He has described this knowledge in two ways: as non-inferential, and as presupposing no other knowledge. One might think the second description adds nothing, since knowledge that logically presupposes other knowledge would seem to have to be inferred from it. His reply is: "This, however, as I hope to show, is itself an episode in the Myth." The whole argument will end by keeping the first and dropping the second.

The picture has to satisfy two requirements that pull against each other. Knowledge at this level is non-inferential, yet it is knowledge. It is ultimate, yet it has authority. And the authority has to show up in how the statement gets made. In Sellars' words: "For where there is no connection between the making of a statement and its authority, the assertion may express conviction, but it can scarcely be said to express knowledge."

> **stop 46** · a bronze statue of blindfolded Lady Justice holding scales · _authority_

How can a statement that is not inferred still have authority? The traditional answer points to observation reports such as "This is red." Their authority comes from being made in just the circumstances in which they are made. That is why they tend to contain token-reflexive words like "this" and "now," which tie what a sentence says to the occasion of saying it.

This gives Sellars a distinction between two kinds of credibility. A token of "Two plus two equals four" gets its credibility from above: it inherits it from its type, all of whose tokens are credible. A token of "This is red" gets its credibility from having come to exist in a certain way, in certain circumstances. So the picture has two ultimate sources of credibility. One is the intrinsic credibility of analytic types, which passes down to their tokens. The other belongs to observation tokens, "a credibility which flows from tokens to types."

> **stop 47** · a printer's type case filled with rows of metal letter sorts · _token and type_

Section thirty-three examines how the tradition explains this authority. It models observation reports on analytic statements. An ordinary empirical statement can be made correctly and still be false. An observation report, the claim goes, is like an analytic statement: being correctly made is both necessary and sufficient for its truth. From this it was concluded, somewhat hastily in Sellars' view, that correctly making the report "This is green" is a matter of following the rules for the use of "this," "is," and "green."

Sellars makes three comments. First, "report" here is a technical term, the Konstatierung of Moritz Schlick and the Vienna Circle. A report in this sense need not be spoken aloud and need not be made by someone to someone. It can be a covert episode, and Sellars notes that not every covert verbal episode is a case of talking to oneself, a point he will come back to at the end of the essay.

> **stop 48** · a portrait photograph of Moritz Schlick · _Konstatierung_

Second, the view under examination treats these reports as actions, so that their correctness becomes the rightness of an action. Sellars objects to that assimilation: "not all ought is ought to do, nor all correctness the correctness of actions." This will matter shortly.

Third, if "following a rule" is taken seriously, rather than as mere regularity, then what brings about the action is the agent's knowledge or belief that the circumstances are of a certain kind, not just the fact that they are. Otherwise lightning followed by thunder would count as following a rule.

Section thirty-four draws the consequence. Suppose reports are actions, their correctness is the correctness of actions, and their authority is that of following a rule in the proper sense. Then a report's authority must rest on a prior awareness that the circumstances are thus and so. That awareness is non-verbal and has intrinsic authority. It is self-authenticating, and the verbal report merely expresses it. This is givenness in its most straightforward form.

> **stop 49** · a nineteenth-century illustration of the world elephant standing on the back of a giant turtle · _tortoise and elephant_

Sellars describes it this way: "These self-authenticating episodes would constitute the tortoise on which stands the elephant on which rests the edifice of empirical knowledge." He adds that the essence of the view is the same whether the episodes are awarenesses of sense contents or awarenesses of how physical objects look.

What is the alternative? Section thirty-five brings back the thermometer view from Part Seven, which Price criticized and Sellars has already rejected, and tries to revise it. On this view a token of "This is green" expresses observational knowledge if it manifests a reliable, acquired tendency to produce such tokens when, and only when, a green object is being looked at in standard conditions. That is a uniformity, like thunder following lightning, except that it is an acquired causal feature of the speaker. Two hurdles stand in its way.

> **stop 50** · an antique mercury thermometer mounted on a carved wooden plaque · _reliable tendency_

The first hurdle is authority, and it can be cleared. The report's authority can consist in the fact that others can infer the presence of a green object from someone's making it. Its correctness can be that of an instance of a general mode of behavior that a linguistic community has reason to sanction and support. This is where the second comment pays off. The correctness belongs to a pattern the community endorses, not to an individual action performed by following a rule.

The second hurdle, Sellars says, "is, however, the decisive one." To express knowledge, the authority "must in some sense be recognized by the person whose report it is." So the reporter needs the concept of green, the concept of uttering "This is green," and the concept of standard conditions. In his words, "the perceiver must know that tokens of 'This is green' are symptoms of the presence of green objects in conditions which are standard for visual perception."

> **stop 51** · a surveyor's brass theodolite on a wooden tripod · _second hurdle_

Section thirty-six accepts this demand instead of backing away from it. Sellars says the requirement is not absurd, and that something very like it is true. As a matter of simple logic, then, no one could observe any fact without knowing many other things as well. It is not enough to grant that observation needs a lot of know-how. The point is about knowing that: knowing general facts of the form "X is a reliable symptom of Y." This, he says, would be "anathema to traditional empiricists," because on their view such generalizations can only be learned from particular observations made earlier.

Now we can see why section thirty-two insisted on the distinction. The report "This is green" is still non-inferential. Jones does not arrive at it by reasoning from a premise about his own reliability. But it does presuppose other knowledge. The two descriptions have separated, and the foundationalist needed them to go together.

> **stop 52** · a medieval manuscript illumination of a scholar reading at a lectern · _non-inferential_

The obvious objection is a regress. Observational knowledge presupposes knowledge of reliability. Knowledge of reliability presupposes earlier observational knowledge, which presupposes earlier knowledge of reliability, and so on without end. Sellars says the charge rests on "too simple, indeed a radically mistaken, conception" of what we say about Jones when we say he knows. Then comes the sentence the essay is best known for. In Sellars' words: "The essential point is that in characterizing an episode or a state as that of knowing, we are not giving an empirical description of that episode or state; we are placing it in the logical space of reasons, of justifying and being able to justify what one says."

This picks up the aside in section five, where Sellars said that analyzing epistemic facts without remainder into non-epistemic ones is a mistake of a piece with the naturalistic fallacy in ethics. To call something knowledge is to give it a normative standing, not to describe it.

> **stop 53** · a mosaic floor laid out in an interlocking geometric grid · _space of reasons_

Much later philosophy starts from this sentence. Robert Brandom reads it as the basis for a two-ply account of observation. The first ply is a reliable differential responsive disposition, which a thermometer or a trained parrot also has. The second is the ability to treat one's own response as a reason, a move in the game of giving and asking for reasons. The second ply is what a thermometer lacks. Brandom uses this to criticize reliabilism, the externalist view associated with Alvin Goldman on which reliability alone can make a belief knowledge, whether or not the knower has access to that reliability. Brandom's reply is that reliability without the second ply is not yet knowledge.

> **stop 54** · a portrait photograph of a gray parrot perched on a wooden stand · _two-ply_

John McDowell draws a different contrast from the same sentence: the space of reasons against the realm of law, the domain of natural-scientific explanation. He insists that the space of reasons is sui generis, meaning it cannot be reduced to the realm of law. Donald Davidson's slogan, which Brandom's study guide quotes, pushes in yet another direction: "Nothing can count as a reason for holding a belief except another belief."

Section thirty-seven dissolves the regress. All the view requires is that Jones now knows the reliability generalization. That in turn requires that he can now cite particular past facts as evidence, which means that he now knows, and so remembers, that those facts obtained. It does not require that he knew them when they obtained. As Sellars puts it: "And the regress disappears."

> **stop 55** · an old photograph of a child's hand pointing at a colored picture book · _now-knowledge_

Jones's present ability to give inductive reasons rests on a long history of acquiring verbal habits in perceptual situations. That history includes utterances of "This is green" that looked like knowledge on the surface. None of them had to be knowledge at the time. Sellars then adds, in parentheses: "At this point, the reader should reread Section 19 above." Section nineteen made the same point about concepts. One can have the concept of green only by having a whole battery of concepts, acquired piecemeal over a long history, and yet in an important sense one has none of them until one has them all. Section thirty-seven makes the parallel point about knowledge. Read together, the two sections suggest that conceptual holism and epistemic holism are one thesis.

> **stop 56** · a botanical engraving of a seed germinating, showing roots and first leaves · _Section nineteen_

Section thirty-eight names the heart of the Myth. It is the idea that observation is constituted by self-authenticating non-verbal episodes whose authority passes to verbal performances made in conformity with semantic rules. These takings are, in Sellars' phrase, "the unmoved movers of empirical knowledge."

He does not deny that observations are inner episodes, or even that they are, strictly speaking, non-verbal. He claims that the sense in which they are non-verbal, the same sense in which thoughts are, gives no support to givenness. This is a promissory note, and Jones pays it off in Parts Fifteen and Sixteen. Nor does he say that empirical knowledge has no foundation, which would put it in a box with rumors and hoaxes. Observation reports do not rest on other propositions in the way other propositions rest on them. But in Sellars' words, "if there is a logical dimension in which other empirical propositions rest on observation reports, there is another logical dimension in which the latter rest on the former."

> **stop 57** · an ouroboros drawing of a serpent swallowing its own tail from an alchemical manuscript · _Hegelian serpent_

The foundation metaphor goes wrong mainly because it is static. It forces a choice between "an elephant which rests on a tortoise," which leaves the question of what holds up the tortoise, and "a great Hegelian serpent of knowledge with its tail in its mouth," which leaves the question of where it begins. Sellars' verdict: "Neither will do." The serpent is coherentism, of the kind later associated with Davidson, and Sellars refuses it as firmly as the tortoise. McDowell later presses this point, arguing that coherentism loses rational friction with the world. Otto Neurath's image is a close relative: we are like sailors who must rebuild their ship on the open sea, plank by plank, with no dry dock to start from.

Sellars' own conclusion is this: "For empirical knowledge, like its sophisticated extension, science, is rational, not because it has a foundation but because it is a self-correcting enterprise which can put any claim in jeopardy, though not all at once."

That mention of science is the hinge. Part Nine changes key and turns to the philosophy of science.

## U8 · Science and ordinary usage  
_959 words · ≈6.4 min_

Part eight ended with knowledge described as rational not because it has a foundation, but because it is a self-correcting enterprise. Part nine opens with a change of key. Sellars turns, apparently abruptly, to the philosophy of science, and the turn is not a digression. He begins with an observation about labels. Classificatory schemes have practical consequences: nominal causes, he says, have real effects. So long as there was no subject called philosophy of science, every philosopher kept at least one eye on what science was doing. Now that the subject has a name, there is a temptation to leave it to the specialists. In Sellars' words, the temptation is "to confuse the sound idea that philosophy is not science with the mistaken idea that philosophy is independent of science."

> **stop 58** · a Victorian botanical glasshouse full of labelled specimen plants · _philosophy of science_

Section forty explains why that independence is an illusion. If discourse were a map divided into side-by-side sub-maps, and analysis were definition, the business of making little ones out of big ones, then specialists could safely fence off their own gardens. But analysis now means clarifying logical structure in the broadest sense, and discourse turns out to be "a tangle of intersecting dimensions whose relations with one another and with extra-linguistic fact conform to no single or simple pattern." Most interesting concepts live in several dimensions at once. The goal of philosophy, Sellars says, is once again "that articulated and integrated vision of man-in-the-universe" which has always been its aim. That phrase is the seed of his later programme.

> **stop 59** · a stack of antique atlas sheets spread overlapping on a table · _tangle of dimensions_

The specific moral concerns science and common sense. Sellars does not claim that analysing ordinary discourse requires the results or methods of the sciences. His claim is that the scientific enterprise is the flowering of a dimension of discourse already present in what historians call the prescientific stage: the dimension of explaining. Fail to understand that dimension writ large, in science, and you will fail to see its role in ordinary usage, and so fail to grasp the full logic even of the simplest empirical terms. Hold on to this. It is what will let Sellars, three parts from now, treat talk of thoughts and sensations as a theory hatched inside common sense rather than a report of what was given to it.

> **stop 60** · a botanical illustration of a single plant shown from seed to full flower · _flowering of a dimension_

Section forty-one draws the consequence. If science continues a dimension of discourse present from the beginning, there should be a sense in which the scientific picture of the world replaces the common-sense picture. But there is a right way and a wrong way to say this. The old slogan that science has shown physical objects aren't really colored is absurd if it means that the sentence "Physical objects have colors" expresses an empirical proposition, widely believed and now shown false. It makes sense only as a clumsy expression of a philosophical rejection of the whole framework of physical objects in space and time, in favour of another built around different categories. It is not like "There are no nonhuman featherless bipeds," a claim made within the framework. And the rejection need not be practical. Within the framework we still use, it remains incorrect to say that nothing is colored.

> **stop 61** · Diogenes holding up a plucked chicken, the featherless biped · _framework_

Then comes the sentence the essay is most often quoted for, after the space of reasons. Speaking as a philosopher, Sellars says, he is prepared to call the common-sense world of physical objects unreal, or, less paradoxically, to say "that in the dimension of describing and explaining the world, science is the measure of all things, of what is that it is, and of what is not that it is not." It inverts Protagoras: not man but science is the measure. Notice the restriction, though. The measure holds in the dimension of describing and explaining, which leaves room for dimensions that are not descriptive at all, such as the one opened in section thirty-six. In a later essay, "Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man," from 1962, Sellars names the two pictures the manifest image and the scientific image, and sets philosophy the task of fusing them into a single stereoscopic vision.

> **stop 62** · a classical bust of Protagoras · _scientia mensura_

Rorty used these two sections to divide Sellars' heirs. Those who take section thirty-six as the lesson, the irreducibility of the space of reasons to empirical description, include Brandom and Rorty himself, and McDowell is usually counted with them. Those who take section forty-one, the priority of the scientific image, include Paul Churchland, Ruth Millikan, and Daniel Dennett. The division is useful because it names a real tension. The restriction in the scientia mensura suggests how Sellars would reconcile them: if knowing is not an empirical description, as section thirty-six insists, then it does not compete with science for the office of saying what there is. Whether that compatibility holds is one of the questions this essay leaves to its readers.

> **stop 63** · a weathered wooden signpost at a fork in a country road · _two kinds of heir_

Section forty-three names the opponent. Many philosophers think that the way we learn our descriptive vocabulary, through an ostensive tie to the world, gives the common-sense categories an unchallengeable authenticity. It follows that theoretical entities, molecules and fields, are mere "calculational devices," valuable only for systematizing observable generalizations. Sellars says this rests on two mistakes. The first is a misunderstanding of the ostensive element in learning a language, which is just the Myth of the Given, already exposed. The second, in his words, is "a reification of the methodological distinction between theoretical and non-theoretical discourse into a substantive distinction between theoretical and non-theoretical existence." The second mistake matters most for what follows. That a term entered our language by postulation is a fact about how we came by it, not about whether what it names is real. Section forty-four calls the rejected view the peninsular conception, with science a hobbyist's offshoot from the mainland. Now Sellars returns to the three experiences he left long ago.

## U9 · Private episodes; thoughts  
_564 words · ≈3.8 min_

Section forty-five returns, in Sellars' phrase, "after a long absence," to the three experiences: seeing that the object over there is red, its merely looking red, and its merely looking as though there were a red object there. Part of their similarity is a shared propositional content. The remainder is what philosophers have tried to capture with impressions or immediate experience. Back in sections twenty-one and following, two explanations were on offer: impressions postulated as theoretical entities, or impressions found by inspection as components of the experiences. The first was set aside as paradoxical. The second has now failed, because it is the Myth of the Given. And if only physical objects are literally red, an impression of a red triangle can be picked out only by a definite description, as whatever the three cases share, which leaves it no better off than a code.

> **stop 64** · a red triangle painted on a white card, beside a red lamp · _after a long absence_

Then the problem deepens. Psychological nominalism says that even simple color concepts are the fruit of long public training on public objects. But then we cannot acquire the concept of a sort of thing by noticing instances of it. In Sellars' words, "to have the ability to notice a sort of thing is already to have the concept of that sort of thing, and cannot account for it." So the puzzle is no longer only how we could come by the idea of a sensation. It is how we could come by the idea of something's looking red, or of seeing that something is red. The general problem is how there can be inner episodes that combine privacy, since each of us has privileged access to our own, with intersubjectivity, since each of us can in principle know about the other's.

> **stop 65** · a child's wooden colour-matching board with painted swatches · _privacy and intersubjectivity_

Sellars notes a linguistic version: a sentence such as "S has a toothache," which anyone can use to state a fact, but only S can use to make a report. That formulation is useful but misses the episodic character of the items. Philosophers who happily allow behavioral dispositions that only we can report balk at non-behavioral episodes. Ryle calls such episodes a category mistake. Others grant them but hold them inexpressible in a language learned among public objects. Sellars rejects both. Inner episodes, he says, "are quite 'effable' in intersubjective discourse," and he promises to show how. His real target is sensation, but he begins with thoughts. Section forty-six shows why empiricism is stuck: overt speech is too sparse to be all our thinking, while the dispositional account looks covertly circular, since we seem to need the notion of thinking to say what makes behavior intelligent.

> **stop 66** · an antique dentist's chair with brass fittings · _effable_

Section forty-seven offers a revised classical view. The tradition held that thoughts are episodes, neither speech nor verbal imagery, which speech expresses, and that they cannot occur without being known. That last belief came from lumping thoughts together with sensations and itches. Purged of that, the view says we each have "a stream of episodes, not themselves immediate experiences, to which we have privileged, but by no means either invariable or infallible, access." Speech is their natural fruition, and privileged access need not work on a quasi-perceptual model. Sellars adds that his view could equally be called a modified form of the thesis that thoughts are linguistic episodes. That raises two questions: in what sense are they inner, and in what sense linguistic? To answer, Sellars announces a myth of his own.

## U10 · Rylean ancestors; theories and models; methodological behaviorism  
_1176 words · ≈7.8 min_

Sellars calls his myth "anthropological science fiction." Imagine a stage of prehistory in which people speak what he calls a Rylean language. Its basic descriptive vocabulary concerns public properties of public objects in space and time. Its resources are limited, yet its expressive power is very great, because it makes subtle use of conjunction, disjunction, negation and quantification, "but especially of the subjunctive conditional." It also has the vagueness and open texture of ordinary talk. The subjunctive conditional matters because it is Ryle's instrument. On Ryle's account, to say someone is vain or intelligent is to say what they would do if certain circumstances arose. A language rich in such conditionals seems able to say everything about mind that Ryle thinks there is to say.

> **stop 67** · a portrait photograph of Gilbert Ryle · _Rylean language_

The myth begins in the middle of things. We are not puzzled about how people learn to talk about public objects; we are puzzled about how they come to talk of inner episodes. Sellars doubts that many philosophers still believe that subjunctive conditionals, used freely, would let these ancestors say everything we say about thoughts, experiences, and immediate experiences. But the doubt must be earned. And the story is designed to show that the idea that an intersubjective language must be Rylean rests on too simple a picture of how public discourse relates to public objects. So Sellars asks two questions. What resources must be added for these ancestors to recognize each other and themselves as beings that think, observe, and have feelings and sensations? And how could those additions be reasonable?

> **stop 68** · a prehistoric cave painting of human figures around a fire · _myth of our ancestors_

The first enrichment, in section forty-nine, is semantical discourse: the ability to say that the German word "rot" means red, or that "Der Mond ist rund" is true if and only if the moon is round. Sellars repeats the lesson of section thirty-one. A semantical statement is not shorthand for talk about the causes and effects of utterances, though it implies a parallel in those causes and effects, and could not do its job otherwise. Section fifty notes what this buys. Semantic talk about what words mean or refer to has the same structure as talk about what thoughts are about. That tempts a modified Rylean reduction: thought talk as dispositional shorthand, its intentionality borrowed from semantics of speech. Sellars resists the reduction while keeping its insight. He wants to reconcile inner episodes with "the idea that the categories of intentionality are, at bottom, semantical categories pertaining to overt verbal performances." Semantics comes first in the order of explanation, and thought is modeled on it.

> **stop 69** · a bilingual German and English dictionary lying open · _semantical discourse_

Section fifty-one supplies the key tool: the distinction between the language of theory and the language of observation. On the received account, found in Campbell, Carnap, Reichenbach, Hempel and Braithwaite, a theory postulates a domain of entities obeying certain principles, and correlates complexes of them with observable situations. These correlations are, in Sellars' phrase, "temporary bridges," tentative "until further notice." The kinetic theory of gases is the example: pressure and temperature are correlated with statistical measures of molecular populations, and the gas laws come out as theorems. A good theory, on this account, explains established empirical laws by deriving theoretical counterparts of them from a small set of postulates about unobserved entities. Sellars grants that the account throws light on the logical status of theories; its fault is emphasis. Sellars then qualifies this picture almost beyond recognition. It describes a logician's reconstruction, not how theories are actually made.

> **stop 70** · a temporary pontoon bridge of boats across a river · _temporary bridges_

His first qualification: theories are built not as uninterpreted calculi but from models. A model describes a domain of familiar objects behaving in familiar ways, so that we can see how the phenomena would arise if they consisted of such things. And, in Sellars' words, "The essential thing about a model is that it is accompanied, so to speak, by a commentary which qualifies or limits—but not precisely nor in all respects—the analogy between the familiar objects and the entities which are being introduced by the theory." To take a standard illustration, gas molecules are like billiard balls, except in ways the commentary states. Hold on to this pairing of model and commentary. Jones will model thoughts on speech, and add that they are not the wagging of a hidden tongue.

> **stop 71** · billiard balls scattered across green baize · _model and commentary_

The second qualification is, Sellars says, more important. The logistical picture hides the fact that theoretical explanation "did not spring full-blown from the head of modern science." Not all common-sense inference is simple induction from observed A's to all A's. Plain people have always explained their surroundings and one another by positing what they could not see. The truth of the matter, Sellars writes, is that "science is continuous with common sense," and the scientist's ways of explaining are refinements of the plain man's. This is where Part nine pays off. And he adds a caution that is easy to miss: the theory and observation distinction is involved in the logic of concepts of inner episodes, but it would be incorrect to say these are theoretical concepts. Their origin in theory does not settle their present status. The payoff of continuity is this: if positing unseen causes is part of common sense, then a concept born in some prehistoric theory can become as much a part of ordinary usage as the word "red," and, on this picture, no appeal to ordinary usage can rule it out as foreign.

> **stop 72** · Athena springing fully armed from the head of Zeus, on a Greek vase · _continuous with common sense_

Section fifty-two draws the lesson for the word "in." It would be a category mistake to think the inflammability of wood is a hidden burning. But it is a legitimate use of "in" to say that in the stodgy air there are countless molecules in turmoil, and that sense is explained by the sense in which the air is a population of molecules, which in turn is explained by the logic of theory and observation. Not every unobservable episode is the offspring of a category mistake. So Sellars grants his ancestors a second enrichment: crude, sketchy theories that explain why things alike in appearance differ in causal behavior, and the reverse.

> **stop 73** · a bundle of firewood stacked beside an unlit hearth · _the legitimate in_

Sections fifty-three to fifty-five introduce a genius Sellars calls Jones, an unsung forerunner of behaviorism, taken as a methodological thesis. Behaviorism in that sense is not a claim that ordinary mental concepts are analyzable into behavior. That is analytical or philosophical behaviorism. It concerns building new concepts, not analyzing old ones; it need not deny privileged access or introspection. The behaviorist grants that we introspect in common-sense mentalistic terms, and that those terms embody real knowledge; he simply proposes to use them only heuristically, and to build his own concepts from scratch. Nor must its concepts be explicitly defined from behavioral vocabulary. Physics and chemistry impose no such restriction, so behavioristic concepts may be theoretical. Nor need they be physiological from the start. They answer to two roles: explaining their phenomena, and integration into the total picture. Where integration is still unforeseeable, they approach what Sellars calls pure theoretical concepts. Now, in Sellars' words, "we are approaching the time for the central episode in our myth": the theory Jones proposes about what goes on inside us.

## U11 · The logic of private episodes: thoughts  
_1427 words · ≈9.5 min_

The central episode is a theory. Sellars asks us to look again at the Rylean ancestors. They describe one another in a behavioristic language, and only in its non-theoretical part. They can report that a hungry man said "Here is an edible object" and ate it. What puzzles Jones is that his fellows behave intelligently not only when their conduct is threaded on a string of overt speech, when they think out loud, but also when nothing is said at all. So in section fifty-six Jones proposes that overt utterances are the culmination of a process which begins with certain inner episodes. His model for those episodes is overt verbal behavior itself. In Sellars' words, the theory is that "overt verbal behavior is the culmination of a process which begins with 'inner speech.'"

> **stop 74** · Rodin's bronze sculpture The Thinker, seated with chin on hand · _inner speech_

Two cautions follow at once. The first is that inner speech is not verbal imagery. Sellars is emphatic: "It is essential to bear in mind that what Jones means by 'inner speech' is not to be confused with verbal imagery." Jones does not yet even have the concept of an image. This protects the order of the myth. Images, sensations and impressions belong to its second episode, and Sellars will not let them leak into the first. The second caution concerns the explanatory claim, which is sharp. When the hungry man says "Here is an edible object" and eats, the true, theoretical cause of his eating is not the overt utterance but the inner utterance of that sentence. Thoughts earn their place by explaining behavior, just as molecules earn theirs by explaining pressure.

> **stop 75** · a still life of a single ripe apple on a wooden table · _true cause_

Section fifty-seven draws two consequences from the model. Because the inner episodes are modeled on speech, the semantical categories come with them. Jones and his fellows already speak of utterances as meaning this or that, or as being about this or that. Now Jones speaks the same way of the inner episodes. Intentionality enters the theory by analogy with meaning. But recall the lesson of section fifty-one: a theory is a model plus a commentary that restricts the analogy. The commentary hastens to add that these episodes are "not the wagging of a hidden tongue," and that inner speech produces no sounds. The model supplies the structure, and the commentary strips out what does not transfer.

> **stop 76** · an antique anatomical engraving of the human tongue and larynx · _model and commentary_

Section fifty-eight then makes five points briskly. The first is that Jones has only the germ of a theory, open to many developments. It need not take the Socratic or Cartesian form, in which inner speech is the function of a separate substance. The second point answers the charge that such episodes are occult. Jones calls them thoughts, and he grants that they are unobserved, nonempirical and inner. In these respects, though, they are no worse off than the particles of physics. The load-bearing sentence is this: "these episodes are 'in' language-using animals as molecular impacts are 'in' gases, not as 'ghosts' are in 'machines.'" The ghost in the machine is Ryle's image for the Cartesian picture. Sellars grants Ryle his target and denies him his conclusion. There can be inner episodes without a category mistake, because this "in" is the "in" of theory.

> **stop 77** · a Victorian spirit photograph showing a translucent ghostly figure beside a seated sitter · _ghost in the machine_

The thoughts are nonempirical only in the simple sense that they cannot be defined in observational terms. As Sellars puts it, their purity "is not a metaphysical purity, but, so to speak, a methodological purity." Nothing prevents them from turning out, at a later stage, to be physiological. He notes that many would already say they are complex events in the cerebral cortex, working along the lines of a calculating machine. Jones has no such idea.

The third point concerns expression. Overt speech is not to be understood as an instrument for expressing thoughts, the way voluntary movements carry out intentions. Speech can of course be used as a means to an end. But, in his words, "serious errors creep into the interpretation of both language and thought if one interprets the idea that overt linguistic episodes express thoughts, on the model of the use of an instrument." Jones' theory is compatible with the ability to think being acquired along with overt speech, and with inner speech coming to run without its overt culmination only later.

> **stop 78** · a brass nineteenth-century telegraph key on a wooden base · _not an instrument_

The fourth point is the crux. The theory explains meaningful speech by thoughts that are also described in semantical terms. It does not analyze the meaning of speech in terms of the intentionality of thought. Sellars insists that "the semantical characterization of overt verbal episodes is the primary use of semantical terms," and that speech, so characterized, is the model for the inner episodes. A seminar should slow down here. In the order in which we come to understand our concepts, language comes first: we grasp what it is for a thought to be about something by analogy with what it is for a sentence to mean something. In the order of being, thought comes first, because inner episodes are what produce the utterances. Sellars can hold both, because the priority of speech is conceptual and the priority of thought is causal. This inverts the program associated with Grice and later Searle, which takes the intentionality of mental states as basic and derives linguistic meaning from it.

> **stop 79** · a Roman coin showing the two-faced god Janus · _semantics primary_

The fifth point guards the boundary with the second episode. Thoughts are introduced as inner episodes, which is only to say that they are introduced as theoretical episodes, but "they are not introduced as immediate experiences." Jones does not yet have that concept. When it arrives, only the philosophers among the ancestors will suppose that thoughts must be a subset of immediate experiences. That supposition is the mirror image of the assimilation whose history section twenty-five traced. That assimilation, of sensation to thought, was one parent of the mongrel of section seven.

> **stop 80** · Frans Hals' portrait of René Descartes · _not immediate experiences_

Section fifty-nine is the dénouement, and the most important page of the constructive half. Jones teaches his compatriots to use the theory in interpreting one another. Tom, watching Dick, has behavioral evidence that warrants saying, in the language of the theory, that Dick is thinking that p. Using the same evidence, Dick can say "I am thinking that p." And then, with Sellars' parenthesis "need it have?", it turns out that Dick can be trained to give reasonably reliable self-descriptions without observing his own behavior. Jones applauds Dick's avowals when the behavioral evidence supports them and frowns when it does not. The ancestors begin to speak of privileged access. The key sentence is this: "What began as a language with a purely theoretical use has gained a reporting role."

> **stop 81** · a nineteenth-century schoolroom with a teacher standing before rows of pupils · _reporting role_

Sellars states the moral with care. The concepts of thoughts are "primarily and essentially intersubjective, as intersubjective as the concept of a positron." Privileged access is real, but it is a dimension of these concepts which is "built on and presupposes this intersubjective status." Sellars names Skinner, Carnap and Wittgenstein as having rightly stressed that language is learned in intersubjective contexts. He claims his myth shows that this fact is compatible with the privacy of inner episodes. That privacy, however, is not absolute. That behavior is evidence for thoughts is built into the very logic of the concepts, as the behavior of gases is built into the logic of molecule talk. Notice what this delivers against the background of section thirty-six. The self-report is non-inferential, yet it is not self-authenticating. Its authority is conferred by training against public evidence.

> **stop 82** · a cloud-chamber photograph of a curving positron track · _as intersubjective as a positron_

Two lines of descendants read this page differently. One reads Jones as the ancestor of what cognitive scientists later called the theory-theory: our everyday psychology of belief and desire is itself a folk theory, posited to explain behavior. Paul Churchland drew a bold corollary. If folk psychology is a theory, it could be a false one, and eliminative materialism holds that it may be replaced outright by neuroscience, as phlogiston was replaced by oxygen. Sellars himself says only that thoughts might "turn out" to be cerebral. Whether his myth licenses elimination, or only identification, is disputed.

> **stop 83** · an engraving of Lavoisier's laboratory apparatus for the combustion of gases · _theory-theory_

The other line runs through Brandom. He reads the dénouement as a model for how any vocabulary can acquire non-inferential, reporting uses on top of inferential ones: concepts are first mastered in inference, and then trained into reliable responses. On that reading the lesson is less about the metaphysics of mind than about observation in general. It gives a genealogy for the pattern of sections thirty-five and thirty-six. The two readings share one conviction: the privacy of the mental is an achievement of public language, not its foundation. And the same story is about to be told a second time, for impressions.

## U12 · Impressions, and the end of the myth  
_1502 words · ≈10.0 min_

The second episode begins by tying off a thread from the essay's first half. Among the thoughts in Jones' framework, section sixty says, will be perceptions: seeing that the table is brown, hearing that the piano is out of tune. Seeing that something is the case is modeled on reporting that it is. Sellars explicitly recalls section sixteen. To say that Dick saw that the table is green is, in part, to ascribe to him the idea this table is green and to endorse it. There Sellars had written: "If I am permitted to issue this verbal currency now, I hope to put it on the gold standard before concluding the argument." This is the backing. Experiences can contain claims because perceptions are posited on the model of claims, and they inherit their semantical structure.

> **stop 84** · a stack of gold bullion bars in a bank vault · _gold standard_

That reconnects the myth to the problem of section twenty-two: the descriptive state that seeing, qualitative looking and existential looking have in common. It had seemed absurd to call impressions theoretical entities, yet only that interpretation seemed to explain their explanatory power. The account of thoughts shows the way out. Jones develops a crude theory of sense perception. He postulates inner, theoretical episodes, which he calls impressions. They are the end results of physical objects acting on the body, and in particular on the eye.

> **stop 85** · a seventeenth-century engraving from Descartes' Dioptrics showing light rays entering the eye · _impressions_

Section sixty-one makes five points, and each one is exact. First, impressions are "states of the perceiving subject, not a class of particulars." The particulars of common sense are books, turnips, dogs and persons. What tempts us to treat impressions as particulars is the model, which is a domain of inner replicas that share the perceptible characteristics of their source. Sellars insists that the model is the occurrence in perceivers of replicas, not of perceivings of replicas. Taking the perceiving of a red and triangular replica as the model forgets the commentary. Worse, "it smuggles into the language of impressions the logic of the language of thoughts." Seeing is cognitive. Model impressions on seeing, and you have rebuilt the mongrel of section seven.

> **stop 86** · a red triangular pennant flag against a plain sky · _inner replicas_

The second point pays off section twenty-four. There Sellars wanted, for the common descriptive core, "a name for this kind of experience which is truly a name, and not just shorthand for a definite description." Theoretical status supplies one. The predicates of a theory are tied logically to observation predicates, but they do not abbreviate descriptions framed in those terms. When the kinetic theory says molecules have mass, "mass" does not mean "the property which" does such and such. Likewise, "impression of a red triangle" does not simply mean the impression caused by red triangles in standard conditions, though it is logically true that such impressions are so caused.

> **stop 87** · an antique brass balance scale with two pans · _truly a name_

The third point concerns the analogy. The model entities, red and triangular wafers, have familiar qualities. If impressions literally had those qualities, the theory would be incoherent, because it would give non-physical states properties that only physical objects can have. The commentary blocks this. The analogy holds only to an untidy extent, and what it carries over is structure. In Sellars' words: "visual impressions stand to one another in a system of ways of resembling and differing which is structurally similar to the ways in which the colors and shapes of visible objects resemble and differ."

> **stop 88** · Goethe's hand-painted color wheel from his Theory of Colours · _structural analogy_

The fourth point heads off a misreading. One might conclude that the concept is purely formal and gains content only by ostensive definition, so that experience can share its structure but not its content. Sellars replies that this "is but another expression of the Myth of the Given." An impression concept is no more and no less without content than any theoretical concept. It is tied to observation through the whole framework, and has nothing to do with "the epistemological fiction of an 'ostensive definition.'" The later debates about qualia and the inverted spectrum are in view here.

> **stop 89** · Leonardo da Vinci's painting of Saint John the Baptist pointing upward · _ostensive definition_

The fifth point is speculative, and Sellars says there is only space to adumbrate it. To ask how impressions fit with electromagnetic fields mixes the molar framework of behavior theory with the micro-theory of physical objects. The proper question is what would correspond to impressions in a micro-theory of sentient organisms. There, Sellars believes, we would find the particulars that sense-datum theorists claimed to find by analysis, as section twenty-three promised. Sentient organisms would appear there as Space-Time worms. Some of their variables would be shared with merely material things. Others would be peculiar to sentient things, and these would be the counterparts of perceived colors and shapes. That is the cash value of the slogan that colors exist only in the perceiver. It is a critique of common sense from the standpoint of an envisaged science, in the spirit of section forty-one.

> **stop 90** · Santiago Ramón y Cajal's ink drawing of branching neurons · _Space-Time worms_

This point had two sequels. Sellars kept developing these sensory states for the rest of his career, under the name sensa. In what is called the grain argument, pressed especially in the Carus Lectures, he held that the homogeneity of sensed color cannot be built from discrete particles, so a completed science must make room for sensa. McDowell, in his Woodbridge Lectures, raised the opposite worry. He argued that once experience is understood as already conceptual, non-conceptual impressions have no transcendental work left to do. Some readers see impressions as what keeps thought answerable to the world. Others see them as the last residue of the Given.

> **stop 91** · Georges Seurat's pointillist painting A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte · _sensa_

Section sixty-two is the final chapter. As his last service before he vanishes without a trace, Jones teaches his theory of perception. His fellows first draw theoretical conclusions in it. Then he trains them to a reporting use: to say "I have the impression of a red triangle" when, and only when, the theory says they are having one. As with thoughts, these concepts are "primarily and essentially intersubjective," and privileged access is built on their public role. Then Sellars makes the callback to section nine. What the ancestors have acquired is not "just another language"—a "notational convenience" or "code." It has an autonomous logical structure, and contains "an explanation of, not just a code for," how things look. This is the surplus value that a code can never have.

> **stop 92** · a wartime Enigma cipher machine with its lid open · _not a code_

The last paragraph turns on the sense-datum theorist. The particulars of the micro-theory are "the solid core of the sense contents and sense fields of the sense-datum theorist." He has glimpsed that framework, and in his study he has learned to use it as a report language. His error is to mislocate its truth. He "confuses his own creative enrichment of the framework of empirical knowledge, with an analysis of knowledge as it was." The section ends with a pun that compresses the essay: "It is in the very act of taking that he speaks of the given."

> **stop 93** · Rembrandt's painting Philosopher in Meditation, a scholar in a dim study beside a spiral stair · _the act of taking_

Section sixty-three is a single paragraph. "I have used a myth to kill a myth — the Myth of the Given." Is it really a myth, Sellars asks, or does the reader not recognize Jones as "Man himself in the middle of his journey from the grunts and groans of the cave to the subtle and polydimensional discourse of the drawing room, the laboratory, and the study"? In his introduction to this edition, Richard Rorty recalls that this passage prompted a question he once put to Sellars, as a pastiche of an old remark about Spinoza. He asked how a man who chooses to bind "the spirit of Hegel in the fetters of Carnap" shall find readers. Sellars, Rorty reports, was not amused. Rorty reads the passage as linking Jones to Hegel's passage from Nature to Spirit.

> **stop 94** · Jakob Schlesinger's portrait of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel · _spirit of Hegel_

What has been paid? The note of section sixteen is honored. And the two ideas that crossbred in section seven have been separated, each given its proper home. The first idea was unlearned sensory episodes posited to explain perception. It survives as impressions: theoretical states that are non-cognitive and reportable only after a public theory is learned. The second idea was inner episodes that are non-inferential knowings and evidence for everything else. It found its home in Part eight: perceptions are genuinely non-inferential, but their authority lies in the space of reasons. Only their union is lost.

> **stop 95** · a mule standing in a pasture · _two ideas_

The essay leaves its heirs divided on open questions. The first is about experience. Brandom takes observation to be a reliable differential disposition joined to the ability to treat its outputs as reasons. On his view, experience as a distinct epistemic item has little left to do. McDowell takes experience itself to be the passive exercise of conceptual capacities, so that the world constrains thought rationally, not merely causally. Both claim section sixteen. The second question is Rorty's division of the heirs. Brandom and Rorty himself, with McDowell usually counted among them, take their lesson from section thirty-six, the irreducibility of the space of reasons. Churchland, Millikan and Dennett take theirs from section forty-one, science as the measure of all things. Sellars believed one vision could hold both. Whether the spirit and the fetters can be joined is the question he leaves us with.
