# EPM — argument map for the palace walk

Source: Sellars, *Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind* (1956; 1997 Harvard ed. with Rorty's
introduction and Brandom's study guide). Section texts: `source/sections/` (local only).
Numbering quirks in this edition: two sections numbered 9 (9a, 9b) and two numbered 16 (16a,
16b); no section marked 42 in either the PDF or the audiobook text.

Pitch: a graduate seminar on the book. Time is weighted by philosophical importance, not by
section count. Budget: ~150 spoken words per minute. Total ≈ 90 min ≈ 13,500 words.

Each unit lists: sections · minutes (≈ words) · the argument · load-bearing passages · connections
the seminar would draw · bridge to the next unit. Quotations from Sellars must be verbatim from the
section files. Secondary literature: paraphrase with attribution; quote only where the wording is
certain (the Davidson slogan below is quoted in Brandom's guide in this edition).

---

## U0 · Opening · 1.5 min (≈ 220 w) · agent A
What the essay is and why it matters: a 1956 lecture-length argument that the whole "framework
of givenness" — not just sense data — is a myth; the source of the phrases "the Myth of the Given"
and "the logical space of reasons." Rorty's introduction names it with Quine's "Two Dogmas" and Wittgenstein's
*Investigations* as the works that turned analytic philosophy; Quine attacked the rationalist
form of foundationalism (analytic/synthetic), Sellars the empiricist form (given/added by mind). The shape of the whole:
a critique of givenness (I–VIII), a turn to science and its relation to common sense (IX), and a
constructive myth — Jones — that explains how talk of inner episodes can be intersubjective and
yet privileged (XII–XVI). Do not summarise the conclusions; set the questions.

## U1 · Part I, §§1–7 · The framework of givenness · 8 min (≈ 1200 w) · agent A
- §1: Nobody denies the difference between inferring and seeing; "the given" as epistemological
  shoptalk carries "a substantial theoretical commitment." Many things have been called given
  (sense contents, material objects, universals, propositions, first principles). The target is
  the *framework*, which Sellars finds even in Kant and "not even Hegel, that great foe of
  'immediacy.'" Sense data are "only … a first step in a general critique."
- §§2–4: Act/object analysis of sensing; being sensed is a relational property of a sense content.
  The key logical point (§3): what is known, even non-inferentially, is *facts* (something's being
  thus-and-so), but what sense-datum theorists say is sensed is *particulars*. Dilemma (a)/(b).
  §4: the theorist "seeks to have his cake and eat it" by using "know" in two senses; knowledge
  by acquaintance as a congealed metaphor (Russell).
- §5: The entailment from sensing to non-inferential knowing, "thrown out the front door," sneaks
  back in. Load-bearing aside: the idea that epistemic facts can be analyzed "without remainder"
  into non-epistemic facts is "a radical mistake — a mistake of a piece with the so-called
  'naturalistic fallacy' in ethics." (Seminar point: this is the seed of §36; Brandom reads EPM's
  core as the irreducibility of the normative/epistemic to the descriptive.)
- §6 (heavy): The inconsistent triad. A: sensing red sense content s entails non-inferentially
  knowing s is red. B: the ability to sense is unacquired. C: the ability to know facts of the
  form x is φ is acquired. Walk the three exits and their prices: drop A (sensing becomes
  non-cognitive, cannot constitute knowledge); drop B (sever sense data from sensations, tickles,
  itches); drop C (violence to empiricism's nominalism). Brandom's guide notes that Sellars'
  dismissal of the third option can mislead readers into thinking he endorses empiricism.
- §7 (heavy): The diagnosis — the classical sense datum is "a mongrel resulting from a
  crossbreeding of two ideas": (1) unlearned inner episodes (sensations) posited in scientific
  explanation of perception; (2) inner episodes that are non-inferential knowings and serve as
  evidence for everything else. How they blend: the grammar of "sensation *of* a red triangle"
  mimics "thought of a celestial city"; sensations seem closer to the mind; and it "doesn't make
  sense to speak of unveridical sensations" — overlooking that veridical/unveridical go together.
  Result: "a sensation of a red triangle is the very paradigm of empirical knowledge."
- Connections: C. I. Lewis (*Mind and the World-Order*) and Price as the living targets; Moore's
  "Refutation of Idealism" (named in §5) and Russell on acquaintance; Rorty (*Philosophy and the
  Mirror of Nature*) generalizes the attack on privileged representations; McDowell (*Mind and
  World*) frames the modern debate as an oscillation between the Given and a coherentism that
  loses friction with the world. Keep the promissory character explicit: the two ideas will be
  separated and each rehabilitated — (1) in §§60–62 (impressions as theoretical states), (2) in
  the critique of foundations (§§32–38).
- Bridge: before examining the two ideas, Sellars detours through a "heterodox" view.

## U2 · Part II, §§8–9b · Another language? · 3 min (≈ 450 w) · agent A
- §8: Ayer's suggestion: sense-datum talk is a contrived language stipulated to have the force of
  "x looks φ to S" — no increase in content. The code picture: code symbols are units; their
  logical relations are "completely parasitical." Enriched code: mnemonic parts ("flags").
- §9a: If it's a code there are no independent logical relations between sense-datum
  'sentences'; ordinary logical words inside them lose their "full-blooded status" (the pink /
  determinate-shade-of-pink example).
- §9b (heavy): Codes vs theories. Both are contrived and "under the control of" the language they
  coordinate with, but a theory has autonomy and "surplus value" — "it is just because theories
  have 'surplus value' that they can provide explanations." The another-language view wants both
  the code's lack of surplus value and a theory's explanatory force; these are incompatible.
  Plant: this code/theory contrast returns in §62 where Jones's language of impressions "is not
  'just another language' … a 'code.'"
- Bridge: whatever sense data are, they are meant to illuminate "looks"; so examine "looks."

## U3 · Part III, §§10–20 · The logic of "looks" · 14 min (≈ 2100 w) · agent B
The first great constructive argument; the seminar's longest session.
- §10: Recap of the two ideas. Crucial widening: the Myth is not essentially sense data, nor
  essentially inner episodes; one can reject private episodes on Wittgensteinian public-learning
  grounds and still fall to "the givenness of such facts as that physical object x looks red to
  person S at time t."
- §11: Looking construed as a triadic relation R(x, S, quality), then "is it analyzable?"
  Analysis vs explanation (Broad's elliptical penny). Theories of appearing (Chisholm) hold looks
  facts ultimate; Sellars agrees they aren't relations to red items, but for a different reason.
- §12 (heavy): "Red" in "looks red" is the same as in "is red"; being red is "logically prior …
  a logically simpler notion" than looking red. Yet "x is red ≡ x would look red to standard
  observers in standard conditions" is a necessary truth — how, without circularity?
- §13: Two-part way out: show *looks* is not a relation at all; then show why the equivalence is
  necessary without being a definition.
- §§14–15 (tell the story well): John in the necktie shop, electric light, Jim: "you can't see
  what isn't so!" John learns to say "It looks green, but take it outside and see." Tempting
  reading (rejected): he has learned to report a new minimal, safer, objective fact.
- §16a–16b (heavy — the pivot): Seeing that x is green is not just an experience; to call an
  experience a seeing is "to characterize it as, so to speak, making an assertion or claim, and …
  to endorse that claim." "X looks green to Jones" *ascribes* the claim without endorsing it; "x
  merely looks green" rejects it. The promissory note: "If I am permitted to issue this verbal
  currency now, I hope to put it on the gold standard before concluding the argument." "Here
  Nature … puts us to the question." "Sees" as Ryle's achievement word; Sellars prefers "'so it
  is' or 'just so' word, for the root idea is that of truth." Connections: Brandom reads "looks"
  talk as expressing the *withholding* of endorsement — a paradigm of his claim that logical and
  quasi-logical vocabulary makes explicit what we do in giving and asking for reasons; McDowell's
  conceptualism about experience (*Mind and World*; Woodbridge Lectures "Having the World in
  View") starts from exactly this idea that experiences "contain" claims, and he later softens
  it ("Avoiding the Myth of the Given," 2009: experiences have intuitional, not propositional,
  content).
- §17: Qualitative vs existential looking as degrees of endorsement ("The tree looks bent" vs
  "There looks to be a bent tree over there"); generic looks explained (red but no determinate
  shade) — compare believing Cleopatra's Needle is tall without believing it any number of feet.
- §18 (heavy): Looking green presupposes the concept of being green, which involves knowing what
  circumstances to view things in; the equivalence is necessary "not because the right-hand side
  is the definition of 'x is red,' but because 'standard conditions' means conditions in which
  things look what they are."
- §19 (heavy): The holism: "one can have the concept of green only by having a whole battery of
  concepts of which it is one element"; "one has no concept pertaining to the observable
  properties of physical objects in Space and Time unless one has them all." Out of step with
  logical atomism. Connections: Quine's holism (different in kind — Sellars' is about concept
  possession, not confirmation); Brandom's inferentialism takes this as its starting point.
- §20: The atomist's rejoinder: the logical space of physical objects rests on the logical space
  of sense contents. Sellars' self-description: "your incipient Meditations Hegeliennes are
  premature" (the atomist speaking). Rorty and Brandom make much of this Hegelian self-image.
- Bridge: the sense-datum theorist's best remaining question — how can something look red unless
  something red is present to the mind?

## U4 · Part IV (§§21–23) and the start of Part V (§24) · Explaining looks · 6 min (≈ 900 w) · agent C
- §21: The question "How can x look red unless something is red?" has "something to" it. If the
  inner item is red in the same sense as physical objects, categorically different things would
  share the same redness. Everyday explanations ("orange object in such light") are good but not
  the only ones.
- §22 (heavy): Two further explanations: (a) immediate experiences as *theoretical entities*, like
  molecules in kinetic theory — flagged as paradoxical and "laid aside" (plant: this is exactly
  where Jones will end up); (b) finding them as components by inspection. The key distinction:
  seeing / qualitatively looking / existentially looking share *propositional content* and differ
  in endorsement; the residue is *descriptive content*, which looks talk specifies only
  indirectly ("experiences that would be seeings if their propositional contents were true").
- §23: The "surface" gambit — a red two-dimensional expanse as the common content — is "a simple
  equivocation on the phrase 'having a red surface'." "The fundamental grammar of the attribute
  red is physical object x is red at place p and at time t." Such particulars may have a place in
  an ideal scientific picture (pointer to §61(5)), not in an analysis of ordinary discourse.
- §24: The "ing/ed" ambiguity of "experience." We want a *name*, not a definite description, for
  the common descriptive content. "Sensation of a red triangle" is non-extensional, but it need
  not be assimilated to "believes in a divine Huntress" — non-extensional contexts need not be
  mentalistic.
- Bridge: historically, sensation-of *was* assimilated to thought-of.

## U5 · end of Part V (§25, a logical point) and Part VI (§§26–30, a historical point) · Impressions and ideas · 6 min (≈ 900 w) · agent C
- §25: Descartes' "thought" and Locke's "idea" cover sensations; objective being in the
  sensation; Descartes: sensations as confused thoughts of their causes; esse est concipi
  extended to esse est percipi. "I think we would all agree, today, that this assimilation of sensations to thoughts is a mistake."
- §26 (heavy): The Myth invoked to explain direct acquaintance with kinds of experience. It
  splits the problem into (1) how we become aware of an experience as of one sort, and (2) the
  inverted spectrum. Claim: (2) is a perplexity only given the Myth's answer to (1). For the
  classical empiricists, awareness of *determinate* sense repeatables was no problem at all; their
  problem of abstract ideas concerned *determinables*. Locke's "adjunctive theory of
  specification" can't relate red to crimson.
- §§27–28: Berkeley (genus only as "contracted" into a species) and Hume (no occurrent thoughts of
  determinables) — all three presuppose an unacquired awareness of determinate repeatables.
- §29 (heavy): The small twist of Hume that yields *psychological nominalism*: "all awareness of
  sorts, resemblances, facts, etc. … indeed, all awareness even of particulars—is a linguistic
  affair." If association is mediated by awareness of resemblance or of redness, the given has
  just moved. Basic word–world ties are between "red" and red *physical objects*, possibly
  causally mediated by sensations.
- §30: The language learner pictured "in a strange forest on a dark night." The test of a theory
  of language is "thinking in presence" (Price's phrase for its converse). Theories that look
  nominalist about thinking in absence turn out "quite 'Augustinian'" about thinking in presence.
  Connections: Wittgenstein, *Investigations* §1 (the Augustinian picture) and §2 (the builders —
  Sellars' "carrier of slabs").
- Bridge: does psychological nominalism equate thinking with talking?

## U6 · Part VII, §31 · The logic of "means" · 4 min (≈ 600 w) · agent C
- "The primary connotation of 'psychological nominalism' is the denial that there is any
  awareness of logical space prior to, or independent of, the acquisition of a language."
- The "thermometer view" (Price): "red" means red by having predicate syntax plus being a
  response to red things. Its crutch: assimilating "'…' means ——" to a relation like "Cowley
  adjoins Oxford."
- Heavy: "statements of the form '"…" means ———' are not relational statements." "'Rot' means
  red" conveys that a mentioned word "plays the same role in a certain linguistic economy" as the
  used, exhibited word "red." "Und" means *and* vs "rot" means *red*: same sense of "means,"
  different roles. The role of "red" may be "a complicated one indeed."
- Connections: the seed of Sellars' functional-role semantics and later dot-quotation ("Meaning
  as Functional Classification"); Brandom's inferential role semantics descends from here;
  contrast with referential ("Fido"–Fido) theories.
- Bridge: now the question the title of Part VIII asks.

## U7 · Part VIII, §§32–38 · Does empirical knowledge have a foundation? · 13 min (≈ 1950 w) · agent D
The second great argument; source of "the logical space of reasons."
- §32 (heavy): The foundationalist picture: a stratum of facts each non-inferentially known and
  presupposing no other knowledge, forming "the ultimate court of appeals." Sellars flags that
  "non-inferential" and "presupposing no other knowledge" come apart — the conflation "is itself
  an episode in the Myth." Authority must be *expressed* in the making of the statement. Token vs
  type credibility; two ultimate modes: analytic types, and observation tokens whose credibility
  "flows from tokens to types."
- §33: Observation reports modeled on analytic statements: correctly made ⇒ true, correctness as
  "following the rules for the use of 'this,' 'is' and 'green.'" Three comments: Konstatierung
  (Schlick's term) as a technical, possibly covert, report; correctness need not be the rightness
  of an action ("not all ought is ought to do"); genuine rule-following requires *knowing* the
  circumstances are of a kind — else lightning–thunder "follows a rule."
- §34 (heavy): So construed, we get "givenness in its most straightforward form":
  self-authenticating non-verbal episodes of awareness — "the tortoise on which stands the
  elephant on which rests the edifice of empirical knowledge." Same whether the episodes are
  about sense contents or about how physical objects look.
- §35 (heavy): The alternative: a Konstatierung "This is green" as a manifestation of a reliable
  acquired tendency (the thermometer view again) — revised. First hurdle: authority as
  inferability of green objects from reports; correctness as socially sanctioned mode of
  behavior. Second, "decisive" hurdle: the authority "must in some sense be recognized by the
  person whose report it is" — so the perceiver "must know that tokens of 'This is green' are
  symptoms of the presence of green objects in conditions which are standard."
- §36 (heaviest): This implies observational knowledge of any fact presupposes knowing general
  facts of the form "X is a reliable symptom of Y" — "anathema to traditional empiricists." The
  regress charge. The key sentence (quote exactly): "The essential point is that in
  characterizing an episode or a state as that of knowing, we are not giving an empirical
  description of that episode or state; we are placing it in the logical space of reasons, of
  justifying and being able to justify what one says." Connections: Brandom's "two-ply" account
  of observation (reliable differential responsive disposition + the ability to take the
  response as a reason) and his critique of reliabilism (externalism about justification, e.g.
  Goldman) — what a thermometer lacks; McDowell's "space of reasons" vs "realm of law" and his
  insistence that the space of reasons is *sui generis*; Davidson's slogan, quoted in Brandom's
  guide: "Nothing can count as a reason for holding a belief except another belief."
- §37: The regress dissolves: Jones must *now* know (remember) the particular facts that support
  the reliability generalization; he need not have known them *then*. "(At this point, the reader
  should reread Section 19 above.)" — the conceptual holism and the epistemic holism are one.
- §38 (heavy): The heart of the Myth: self-authenticating non-verbal episodes whose authority is
  transmitted to verbal performances — "the unmoved movers of empirical knowledge." Sellars does
  *not* deny observations are inner, even non-verbal, episodes. Nor that there is point to the
  foundation picture: observation reports don't rest on other propositions in the same way —
  "but … there is another logical dimension in which the latter rest on the former." The static
  metaphor forces a choice between "an elephant which rests on a tortoise" and "a great Hegelian
  serpent of knowledge with its tail in its mouth." Close with: "empirical knowledge, like its
  sophisticated extension, science, is rational, not because it has a foundation but because it
  is a self-correcting enterprise which can put any claim in jeopardy, though not all at once."
  Connections: Neurath's boat as a cognate image; coherentism (Davidson) as the serpent Sellars
  also refuses; McDowell's charge that coherentism loses friction.
- Bridge: a change of key — the philosophy of science.

## U8 · Part IX, §§39–44 · Science and ordinary usage · 6 min (≈ 900 w) · agent E
- §39: Philosophy of science as a specialty tempts us "to confuse the sound idea that philosophy
  is not science with the mistaken idea that philosophy is independent of science."
- §40: Discourse is not a map of side-by-side sub-maps but "a tangle of intersecting
  dimensions"; the goal is "that articulated and integrated vision of man-in-the-universe." Science
  is the flowering of a dimension of discourse already present in prescientific usage.
- §41 (heavy): The scientific picture "replaces" the common-sense picture — the right and wrong
  ways to say it. "Physical objects aren't really colored" as a rejection of a framework, not an
  empirical claim within it. The *scientia mensura* (quote exactly): "in the dimension of
  describing and explaining the world, science is the measure of all things, of what is that it
  is, and of what is not that it is not." Connections: the manifest and scientific images
  ("Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man," 1962); Rorty's division of heirs into those who
  take §36 as the lesson (Brandom, McDowell, Rorty himself) and those who take §41 (Churchland,
  Millikan, Dennett) — attribute to Rorty without citing this book.
- §43 (heavy): The "ostensive tie" is thought to guarantee the "unchallengeable authenticity" of
  common-sense categories, making theoretical entities "calculational devices" (instrumentalism).
  Two mistakes: the Myth of the Given; and "a reification of the methodological distinction
  between theoretical and non-theoretical discourse into a substantive distinction between
  theoretical and non-theoretical existence."
- §44: The "peninsular" conception of scientific discourse — rejected.
- Bridge: back to the three experiences, and the question of inner episodes.

## U9 · Parts X–XI, §§45–47 · Private episodes; thoughts · 4 min (≈ 600 w) · agent E
- §45 (heavy): Return "after a long absence." Alternative (b) (inspection) has failed; alternative
  (a) (theoretical entities) was paradoxical. Worse: psychological nominalism makes even "seeing
  that something is red" puzzling — "to have the ability to notice a sort of thing is already to
  have the concept of that sort of thing." The general problem: episodes that combine privacy
  with intersubjectivity. Against Ryle (inner episodes a category mistake) and against the
  ineffability view: inner episodes are "quite 'effable' in intersubjective discourse." Thoughts
  first.
- §46: Empiricism's two minds about thoughts: verbal episodes (too few) vs dispositions to
  intelligent behavior (covertly circular).
- §47: The revised classical view: a stream of episodes "not themselves immediate experiences,"
  to which access is "privileged, but by no means either invariable or infallible"; verbal
  behavior their "natural fruition"; privileged access need not be quasi-perceptual.
- Bridge: in what sense "inner," in what sense "linguistic"? — a myth of Sellars' own.

## U10 · Parts XII–XIV, §§48–55 · Rylean ancestors; theories and models; behaviorism · 8 min (≈ 1200 w) · agent E
- §48: "Anthropological science fiction": a Rylean language — public properties of public
  objects, rich logic, "especially … the subjunctive conditional," vagueness and open texture.
  Begin *in medias res*: the puzzle is not public language but talk of inner episodes.
- §49–50: First enrichment: semantical discourse ("'Rot' means red"; Tarskian truth), not
  shorthand for causes and effects of utterances. Semantic talk has the structure of
  intentionality — tempting a modified Rylean reduction. Sellars' target: reconcile inner
  episodes with "the idea that the categories of intentionality are, at bottom, semantical
  categories pertaining to overt verbal performances."
- §51 (heavy): Theories: postulated domains, correspondence rules as "temporary bridges"; but
  theories are built from *models* plus a *commentary* that qualifies the analogy; and
  hypothetico-deductive explanation "did not spring full-blown from the head of modern science" —
  "science is continuous with common sense." Concepts of inner episodes are "involved" with the
  theory/observation distinction without being theoretical concepts.
- §52: The legitimate "in" of molecules in air vs the category mistake of hidden burning; second
  enrichment: crude theoretical discourse.
- §§53–55: Jones as forerunner of *methodological* (not philosophical/analytical) behaviorism: a
  program for constructing new concepts, not analyzing old ones; theoretical concepts permitted;
  not defined physiologically; the regulative ideal of an integrated picture; "pure theoretical
  concepts."
- Bridge: the central episode.

## U11 · Part XV, §§56–59 · The logic of private episodes: thoughts (Jones, I) · 9 min (≈ 1350 w) · agent F
- §56 (heavy): Jones' theory: intelligent behavior without overt speech is explained by "inner
  speech," modeled on overt speech — not verbal imagery (they don't yet have images).
- §57: Semantical categories carry over to the theoretical episodes; model plus commentary ("not
  the wagging of a hidden tongue").
- §58: Five points: the theory's germ admits Cartesian or physiological developments; thoughts
  are "in" language users "as molecular impacts are 'in' gases, not as 'ghosts' are in
  'machines'"; overt speech is not an instrument for expressing thoughts; the semantics of overt
  speech is *primary* — thoughts' intentionality is modeled on it, not the reverse; thoughts are
  not introduced as immediate experiences.
- §59 (heaviest of the constructive half): The dénouement: Tom ascribes "Dick is thinking that p"
  on behavioral evidence; Dick is trained to self-ascribe without observing his behavior. "What
  began as a language with a purely theoretical use has gained a reporting role." Concepts of
  thoughts are "primarily and essentially intersubjective, as intersubjective as the concept of a
  positron"; privileged access is a dimension "built on and presupposes" the intersubjective
  status; privacy is not "absolute privacy." Connections: the ancestor of the "theory-theory" of
  folk psychology and, through Churchland, of eliminative materialism; Brandom's reading of
  Jones as a model of how reporting (non-inferential) uses of vocabulary can be added to
  inferential ones; Skinner, Carnap and Wittgenstein (named here) on the public learning of language, reconciled with
  inner episodes; Ryle's "ghost in the machine" behind §58's image; how Sellars' order of explanation (language first, thought second in the order of
  knowing; thought first in the order of being) differs from Grice/Searle.
- Bridge: the second episode — impressions.

## U12 · Part XVI, §§60–63 · Impressions (Jones, II) and the end · 9 min (≈ 1350 w) · agent F
- §60: Perceptions (seeing that) are among the thoughts, modeled on reporting that — reconnecting
  to §16's endorsement account. The question from §§22 ff. returns; Jones postulates impressions
  as end results of physical impingements.
- §61 (heavy): (1) Impressions are "states of the perceiving subject, not a class of
  particulars." The model is inner *replicas*, not perceivings of replicas — the latter smuggles
  the logic of thoughts into impressions. (2) Theoretical predicates are not shorthand for
  definite descriptions — this answers §24's demand for a *name*. (3) The commentary: the
  essential analogy is structural — "visual impressions stand to one another in a system of ways
  of resembling and differing which is structurally similar to the ways in which the colors and
  shapes of visible objects resemble and differ." (4) Not "purely formal"; ostensive definition as
  "epistemological fiction." (5) The micro-theory of sentient organisms: its particulars are "the
  solid core" of the sense-datum theorist's sense contents; "Space-Time worms" with variables
  peculiar to sentient things. Connections: later Sellars' "sensa" and the grain argument (Carus
  Lectures); McDowell's objection (Woodbridge Lectures) that Sellars' transcendental role for
  sensory impressions is unnecessary once experience is conceptual.
- §62 (heavy): Jones teaches the theory; a reporting use follows. Impressions, like thoughts,
  "primarily and essentially intersubjective." Explicitly *not* "just another language … a code"
  — callback to §9b. The sense-datum theorist "confuses his own creative enrichment of the
  framework of empirical knowledge, with an analysis of knowledge as it was." Last sentence:
  "It is in the very act of taking that he speaks of the given."
- §63: "I have used a myth to kill a myth." Jones as "Man himself in the middle of his journey."
  Rorty's introduction recalls asking Sellars, about this passage, how one who binds "the
  spirit of Hegel in the fetters of Carnap" shall find readers.
- Close (≈ 1 min): what has been paid on the promissory note of §16; the two ideas of §7
  separated and each given its proper home; the open questions a seminar would leave with
  (McDowell vs Brandom on experience; §36 vs §41).
