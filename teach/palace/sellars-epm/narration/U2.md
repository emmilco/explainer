# U2 · Part II: Another language?

::: narration
Part two examines a heterodox proposal, which Sellars associates with Ayer: that sense-datum talk is another language, contrived by the epistemologist for situations the plain man describes by saying that the book now looks green to him. On this view the vocabulary of sense data adds no descriptive content. A sentence of the form "x presents S with a phi sense datum" is simply stipulated to have the force of "x looks phi to S". So "The tomato presents S with a bulgy red sense-datum" means exactly what "The tomato looks red and bulgy to S" means, because it was stipulated to. Sellars then builds a picture of a code: a system of symbols, each standing for a whole sentence, whose parts are not themselves symbols, and whose logical relations are, in his words, "completely parasitical" on those of the sentences represented.

::: narration
The code can be enriched. Its symbols might contain mnemonic parts, flags whose letters remind us of features of the sentences they stand for. Sense-datum sentences could be such flags, with "sense datum" reminding us that the represented sentence contains "looks," and "red" that it contains "looks red." But taken seriously, this means there are no independent logical relations among sense-datum sentences, and words like "all," "some," "red," and "determinate" inside them lose "the full-blooded status of their counterparts in ordinary usage." The clues can mislead. From "Some elephants are pink" one may infer that some elephants have a determinate shade of pink. But the right to fly the flag "Some sense data are pink" carries no right to fly "Some sense data have a determinate shade of pink," since, as Sellars will argue about looks, something can look pink without looking any determinate shade.

::: narration
Section nine b draws the decisive contrast. One is constantly tempted to treat the flags as sentences in a theory, coordinated with ordinary perception talk as molecule talk is coordinated with talk of gas pressure. Codes and theories do share something: both are contrived, and both are under the control of the language they are coordinated with. That shared feature lends the another-language view its plausibility. But within that control, Sellars says, "the theoretical language has an autonomy which contradicts the very idea of a code." The another-language theorist wants it both ways, drawing on the fact that codes have no surplus value, and on the fact that theories explain. The two are incompatible, "for it is just because theories have “surplus value” that they can provide explanations."

This contrast returns in section sixty-two, where the language of impressions that Jones teaches is indeed another language, but one with an autonomous logical structure, containing an explanation of, and not merely a code for, how things look. Meanwhile, Sellars notes, the another-language theorists spent their ingenuity translating physical-object statements into statements about sense contents. Any clarification a code could offer would have to come from illuminating ordinary talk of looking and appearing, and that runs into trouble once the role of "looks" is understood. In his words, "it is to an examination of this role that I now turn."
